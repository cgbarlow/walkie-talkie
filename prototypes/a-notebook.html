Project notes

Grandad's walkie-talkie

One big button. Hold it and talk, let go and listen. It works over Wi-Fi or mobile data, so the other walkie can be across the room or across the country.

Started November 2026New ZealandFamily build, not a product

Two stages

We test the idea on hardware we already have, then build the version we actually want.

Stage 1 · now

Rabbit R1 proof of concept

Two Rabbit R1s, wiped and reflashed so they run one app and nothing else. The R1 already has a side button, a scroll wheel, a speaker and Wi-Fi, so it is the quickest way to find out if Grandad gets on with it.

Stage 2 · next

ESP32 unit we build ourselves

A small box made from about ten cheap parts: a big orange button, a channel knob, a small screen and a proper speaker. No reflashing, and the battery lasts days instead of hours.

Both stages talk to the same server in the same format, so an R1 and an ESP32 unit can share a channel.

What happens when you press the button

  1. You hold the button. Within a third of a second it chirps, so you know it is ready. It starts recording the moment you press, so your first word is never cut off.
  2. Your voice is compressed, locked with a key that only the walkies share, and sent to a small server in Auckland or Sydney.
  3. The server passes it to every other walkie on the channel. It cannot listen in, because it never has the key.
  4. Their walkie clicks, then plays your voice. When you let go, it plays a short roger beep.

Only one person can talk at a time. If someone else is already talking, you hear a busy tone. If a walkie was off or out of signal, it plays the last message when it comes back, for up to 24 hours.

How long before they hear you?

From your mouth to their ear, through the server and back out.

70 to 130 ms

Estimates from the research, not measurements. The R1's own audio delay is the big unknown and gets measured on the first night.

Made for Grandad

Loud and clear

Older ears lose the high sounds first, where s, f, th and sh live. Incoming speech gets a lift in that range and plays near full volume.

One job only

No menus, no settings, no way out of the walkie app. It looks and sounds the same every day.

Says what it's doing

Turning to a new channel reads its name out, such as "Channel two, Chris". A missed message plays once by itself: "You have a message from Chris".

Lives on a dock

It sits on charge beside his chair. If the battery runs low or it drops offline, the family gets an alert.

Microphone off by default

The microphone is only on while the button is held. There is no way to listen in from afar, and there never will be.

Changeable from afar

Volume, quiet hours and the channel knob can be set remotely, so nobody needs to visit to fix it.

This is not an emergency alarm. In an emergency, call 111. Each walkie comes with a printed card that says so. If Grandad lives alone or is frail, it goes alongside a monitored medical alarm, not instead of one.

      Questions

      Why not just buy a pair of radios?

      If you live within a kilometre or two, a pair of licence-free UHF radios for about $80 is simpler and has almost no delay. They fall down on distance, hills, privacy (anyone can tune in), replaying missed messages and telling family when something is wrong.

      Why start with the R1 if it isn't the final device?

      It already has every part a walkie needs, so we can test the whole idea (the server, the sounds, whether Grandad likes it) without building any hardware. What we learn carries straight over to Stage 2.

      Why move off the R1 later?

      Rabbit stopped making the R1 in 2026. Its battery lasts about a day and a half, it needs reflashing and rooting before it will run our app, and its mobile radio misses New Zealand's 700 MHz band, which matters in the country. An ESP32 unit costs a fraction as much and has none of those problems.

      Is it private?

      Each message is encrypted on the walkie and only decrypted on the other walkies. The server in the middle routes the messages but cannot open them. The microphone is only on while the button is held.

      What does it cost to run?

      The server can run for nothing on a free cloud plan, or for under NZ$10 a month on a paid one. Twenty minutes of talk a day uses about 200 MB of data a month, which only matters if a walkie is on mobile data rather than Wi-Fi.

      Can we add more people?

      Yes. Each channel can hold several walkies, and a message on a channel goes to all of them. Two to ten walkies is a light load for the server.
