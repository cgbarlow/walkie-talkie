# Shrink the relay; you cannot delete it

No option removes every central piece, but the owner can stop maintaining a relay fleet. Meshtastic and other licence-free radio cannot carry live, clear, encrypted voice between towns; Meshtastic's own documentation says its sub-GHz presets are too narrow for continuous audio. Direct peer-to-peer over home Wi-Fi is feasible on the ESP32-S3 and would connect most New Zealand home pairs (a researcher's estimate of 75 to 95%, not a measurement), but the rest need a relay of last resort, and every design still needs rendezvous, firmware updates and something always-on to send offline alerts. The relay's bandwidth is cheap at any scale (cents per family per month); the real burden is on-call availability, activation spikes, the update channel and support, and peer-to-peer removes little of that. The recommended path is to keep the blind relay for Stages 1 and 2, make it replaceable from day one, then add a direct path with a standard TURN or iroh fallback so that a commercial version runs on static files and serverless functions with no server to patch. Reading the interception statute first-hand weakens the earlier hope that a hardware-only vendor escapes it: the Act's definition of a telecommunications service includes equipment, so any vendor is probably a "service provider" with a duty to assist under warrant. That needs a formal opinion before selling.

## What the first-hand check changed

- **Interception law.** The statute defines a telecommunications service as any goods, services, equipment or facilities that enable telecommunication, so selling the radios alone probably makes the vendor a service provider. Decentralising buys little legally.
- **TURN carries the existing protocol.** Cloudflare's TURN is a standard RFC 8656 relay and the app supplies its own encryption, so the sealed Opus-over-UDP packets can use it as a fallback without moving to WebRTC media. Price confirmed: US$0.05/GB after 1,000 GB a month, shared with the media relay.
- **iroh now supports the planned hardware.** Its docs list the ESP32-S3 with PSRAM as able to use relays and address lookup, and paid relays start at US$19 a month.
- **Tailscale's terms bar resale.** Its terms forbid commercially exploiting or reselling use of the service, so a sold device needs a separate agreement.
- **ZeroTier's licence changed.** The agent is now MPL-2.0; only the controller is source-available and non-commercial.
- **esp_peer is Espressif-only.** Its licence permits use only with Espressif chips and it ships prebuilt libraries, so the R1 and laptop client need libpeer (MIT) instead.
- **Voicetastic is faster than estimated.** Recomputed from its published pacing, a 10-second clip takes about 15 seconds per hop, not 20 to 30. Still not live.
- **IPv6 is better than reported for some networks.** APNIC now shows 2degrees at 49%, One NZ at 41% and Starlink at 80% IPv6-capable; Spark is still about 1%.
- **Smaller fixes.** The amateur radio quote comes from the 2025 amateur licence, not the ZL2GT page; Wellington's mesh moved to a faster preset in 2024; LiveKit's ESP32 SDK no longer carries a "developer preview" warning; Nabu Casa's service is now US$6.50 a month.

## Radio mesh cannot carry Grandad's voice to the next town

Meshtastic is a text mesh. Its default LongFast preset runs at **1.07 kbit/s** and even its fastest, ShortTurbo, at 21.88 kbit/s ([Meshtastic blog, Apr 2025](https://meshtastic.org/blog/why-your-mesh-should-switch-from-longfast/)). Each packet carries at most 239 bytes of payload and a hop limit of at most seven ([Meshtastic mesh algorithm](https://meshtastic.org/docs/overview/mesh-algo/)). Its only audio feature is an experimental Codec2 module for 2.4 GHz SX128x radios on two LilyGo boards, and the docs state plainly that the sub-GHz bands are too narrow for continuous audio packets even in the Short and Fast presets ([Meshtastic audio docs, v2.8](https://meshtastic.org/docs/configuration/module/audio/)).

A third-party project, Voicetastic, sends whole voice clips with error recovery; its Codec2 modes run from 1.2 to 3.2 kbit/s with 1.2 as the default Codec2 mode ([Voicetastic wiki](https://github.com/voicetastic/voicetastic-core/wiki/Voice-Protocol)). On LongFast it uses 96-byte chunks and waits 900 ms between packets, so a 10-second clip at 1.2 kbit/s (1,500 bytes, 16 packets) takes about 15 seconds to cross one hop before any loss recovery, and each relaying hop competes for the same channel (corrected: the first pass estimated 20 to 30 seconds; this is still the researcher's arithmetic, not a measurement). Its current protocol also dropped its own encryption and relies on Meshtastic's shared channel key. Codec2 at these rates is a robotic-sounding vocoder, a real risk for a listener with hearing loss, against the project's 16 to 24 kbit/s Opus. No study of Codec2 for hearing-impaired listeners was found.

Riding the community mesh is not an answer either. Wellington's mesh passed 150 active nodes by mid-2024, with channel use at busy sites peaking above 65%, and its operators moved the whole mesh from LongFast to ShortFast at the end of August 2024 to cope ([Meshtastic blog](https://meshtastic.org/blog/why-your-mesh-should-switch-from-longfast/); the forum thread cited earlier could not be opened). Gisborne District Council found technical limitations with Meshtastic before adopting MeshCore as one of at least six backup modes in September 2025 (council release now offline; confirmed by [Gisborne Herald](https://www.nzherald.co.nz/gisborne-herald/news/self-taught-local-develops-emergency-communication-network-with-tairawhiti-authorities/S5XX55RHTFAQHMRF5II3D2KYKM)). These are volunteer text networks built on hilltop repeaters; coverage outside a few regions cannot be promised to buyers.

The alternatives fail on at least one requirement each. Reticulum MeshChat lists Codec2 audio calls as a beta feature, tested by its author over LoRa with a single hop ([reticulum-meshchat](https://github.com/markqvist/reticulum-meshchat)). Purpose-built ESP32 plus SX1262 walkie-talkies exist, such as LILYGO's T3-S3 MVSR, whose example uses Codec2 at 3,200 bit/s over FSK at 914.6 MHz ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2025/03/28/a-lora-based-walkie-talkie-meet-lilygo-t3-s3-mvsr-lora-voice-communication-kit/)); that frequency sits just below New Zealand's 915 to 928 MHz band, and no range is published, so "a street or suburb, not the next town" remains an inference. Amateur radio is ruled out because the amateur licence says communications must not be encoded to obscure their meaning ([Amateur GURL Notice 2025](https://gazette.govt.nz/notice/id/2025-go3272); corrected source). Amazon Sidewalk runs in the US, Canada and Mexico, with Europe planned for 2026 and no mention of New Zealand ([Semtech Sidewalk FAQ](https://www.semtech.com/amazon-sidewalk-faqs)). Cellular (LTE-M, Cat-1) removes the home Wi-Fi dependence but swaps the relay for a carrier and still needs a server.

Legally, the current Short Range Devices licence (2022, still listed as current by RSM) allows 915 to 928 MHz at up to 1 W EIRP, and 920 to 928 MHz at up to 4 W with frequency hopping or digital modulation, under Special Condition 23's out-of-band limits; it sets no duty cycle for that band and says nothing about encryption or content ([Gazette 2022-go3100](https://gazette.govt.nz/notice/id/2022-go3100)). RSM reminded LoRa users of Condition 23, the R-NZ label and the Supplier's Declaration of Conformity in August 2026 ([RSM](https://www.rsm.govt.nz/about/news-and-updates/lorawan-and-compliance-with-general-user-radio-licence-gurl-conditions)). Short range and Wi-Fi devices sit at RSM's lowest compliance level: meet the standard, describe the product, sign a declaration and label it, with each significant variant needing its own declaration ([RSM compliance guide](https://www.rsm.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/documents/compliance-guide.pdf)). Radio is credible only as an optional fallback: ESP-NOW between units on the same property costs nothing in hardware, and an optional LoRa add-on could carry the tiny offline and low-battery alerts.

## Direct peer-to-peer reaches most NZ homes, never all

The ESP32-S3 can do this. Espressif's `esp_peer` provides ICE, STUN and TURN with Opus audio in under 60 KB of RAM in a minimal setup ([esp_peer README](https://components.espressif.com/components/espressif/esp_peer/versions/1.5.8/readme)), but its licence allows use only with Espressif chips and it ships as prebuilt per-chip libraries ([esp_peer licence](https://components.espressif.com/components/espressif/esp_peer/versions/1.5.8/license?language=en)). That suits the ESP32 firmware, not the R1 app or the laptop test client, which should use the MIT-licensed libpeer it derives from, which supports STUN, TURN, IPv4 and IPv6 on Linux ([libpeer](https://github.com/sepfy/libpeer)). The researcher's view is that the lowest-risk route keeps the existing sealed-Opus-over-UDP packets and adds only a small hole-punching layer, because the project already has its own encryption.

The obstacle is New Zealand's broadband mix. **2degrees moves every dynamic-IP home connection behind carrier-grade NAT**, with a static address at NZ$10 a month, though it also gives every connection IPv6 by default ([2degrees](https://2degrees.nz/help/broadband-help/ip-addresses/2degrees-ip-addresses)). Fixed wireless is 20% of connections and low-earth-orbit satellite 3% nationally and 19% of rural connections ([TCF Annual Report 2025](https://www.tcf.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/TCF-Annual-Report-2025-Final-1.pdf), citing the Commerce Commission). Starlink's default IPv4 is carrier-grade NAT with no inbound traffic, a public IPv4 address is offered only on its Priority plans, and its own routers support no port forwarding or firewall rules for IPv4 or IPv6 ([Starlink support](https://starlink.com/support/article/1192f3ef-2a17-31d9-261a-a59d215629f4)).

IPv6 helps more than first reported. APNIC's live figures, read on 15 November 2026, put 2degrees at 49% IPv6-capable, One NZ at 41%, Starlink at 80% and Spark at about 1%, with New Zealand overall near 34% ([APNIC](https://stats.labs.apnic.net/ipv6/NZ)). IPv6 removes address translation but not home firewalls, so two IPv6 units still need to open a path to each other at the same time. Spark's help page was behind a bot check; a search extract shows it naming carrier-grade NAT for its wireless broadband only. No statement was found for Spark fibre or One NZ fibre in 2026.

Carrier-grade NAT does not by itself block hole punching; what matters is how the outer NAT assigns ports. Measured success elsewhere brackets the answer: **70% ± 7%** for the hole-punching stage across 4.4 million libp2p attempts on 85,000 networks in 167 countries ([arXiv 2510.27500](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27500v1)), and "well north of 90%" in Tailscale's own metrics, where every connection starts through a relay and stays there if punching fails ([Tailscale, Oct 2025](https://tailscale.com/blog/nat-traversal-improvements-pt-1)). The researcher's estimate for NZ home pairs is **75 to 95% direct**, built by multiplying figures from different sources; no measurement of NAT behaviour on any NZ network was found. The arXiv authors published their dataset, which may include New Zealand networks and is the cheapest next check. A separate researcher estimated that only 35 to 55% of NZ homes could accept an inbound connection with no customer action, which is why "one unit in the family acts as the server" cannot be the only path. Rest homes, hospitals and guest Wi-Fi that block UDP matter for this audience too.

Three small services survive the move to peer-to-peer. Address discovery (STUN) is available free; Cloudflare's is free and unlimited ([Cloudflare TURN FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/turn/faq/)). Rendezvous, the way two units learn each other's current address, can be a stateless mailbox of sealed blobs: far smaller than a media relay, and a compromise reveals only IP addresses and timing. The relay for pairs that cannot punch through is the part that cannot vanish. The choices are to screen homes at set-up and accept failures, let a well-connected family unit forward for the others (which does nothing for a two-unit pair that both sit behind hard NAT), or buy fallback per gigabyte. Cloudflare's TURN is the simplest per-gigabyte option: it relays any UDP payload, leaves encryption to the application, and issues short-lived credentials from an API, so it needs only a small credential function. It relays over UDP and IPv4 only, so it does not rescue networks that block UDP.

Groups work without a server. The 3GPP off-network floor-control procedure is a ready model: request the floor, wait briefly for objection, then announce "floor taken" ([3GPP TS 24.380](https://www.3gpp.org/DynaReport/24380.htm); the annex page cited earlier is offline and was not re-read). A ten-unit group has 45 pairwise paths, so the mesh must tolerate missing links by forwarding through a member both sides can reach.

Security shifts instead of improving. Each member's home IP address becomes visible to the others, and the convenient central point for re-keying and dropping a lost unit disappears; the replacement is a membership list signed by an owner unit and passed between units, which is more firmware to write. The worst consumer incident in this class came from a peer-to-peer platform, not a relay: ThroughTek's Kalay let an attacker who obtained a device's ID, and knew the protocol, register as that device and reach live audio and video, on a platform claiming 83 million devices ([Mandiant, Aug 2021](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/mandiant-discloses-critical-vulnerability-affecting-iot-devices)). The lesson applies to any rendezvous service: units must prove identity with their key pair, not an ID. One researcher's conclusion was blunt, and it is the fair one: for this threat model a hardened blind relay is the lower-risk design, and peer-to-peer is a resilience and cost optimisation.

## Borrowed networks and customer boxes move the burden

Riding someone else's network sounds like the escape, and the research found no shipped consumer voice device that does it. iroh is the strongest candidate. Its docs now list the ESP32-S3 with PSRAM, which the Stage 2 design already specifies, as able to use relays and address lookup; without PSRAM it works only on the local network ([iroh compatibility](https://docs.iroh.computer/compatibility)). Its free public relays are for development and hobby use only, with no uptime guarantee and shared rate limits ([iroh public relays](https://docs.iroh.computer/iroh-services/relays/public)); the paid plan is US$19 a month for 10,000 concurrent connections, then US$0.003 per extra endpoint a month, with 100 GB of relayed traffic then US$0.09/GB, and direct traffic is not counted ([iroh pricing](https://www.iroh.computer/pricing)). It speaks QUIC, so the sealed packets would ride iroh datagrams rather than raw UDP.

The other overlays are weaker. An unofficial MIT-licensed Tailscale client for the ESP32-S3, MicroLink, now calls itself production-ready and reports 30 to 150 ms round trips through Tailscale's relays, but takes 15 to 20 seconds from boot to connected and is not affiliated with Tailscale ([MicroLink](https://github.com/CamM2325/microlink)). Tailscale's terms forbid commercially exploiting any part of the service or reselling use of it, so units sold on its network would need a separate agreement ([Tailscale terms, s 2.3](https://tailscale.com/terms)). ZeroTier's agent is now MPL-2.0; its controller and other "nonfree" parts are source-available for non-commercial use only, so a product would use ZeroTier's hosted controller or license the self-hosted one ([ZeroTier licence](https://github.com/zerotier/ZeroTierOne/blob/dev/LICENSE.txt); corrected, the forum thread cited earlier is gone). Tor adds seconds of delay. HiveMQ's public broker is for quick tests and not for private or production data ([HiveMQ](https://www.hivemq.com/public-mqtt-broker/)). Free shared back ends also change terms once load grows: matrix.org introduced paid accounts and fair-use limits in July 2025 ([Matrix](https://matrix.org/blog/2025/07/terms-update/)) and Meshtastic restricted its public MQTT server in July 2024 ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Enhancements-coming-soon-to-Meshtastic-s-public-server.862237.0.html)). A fleet that only consumes volunteers' bandwidth is the pattern that gets rate-limited. Nostr relays and the BitTorrent DHT could serve as a rendezvous channel nobody owns, though no ready ESP32 DHT client was found.

Customer hosting has the same shape. Tailscale, Plex, Synology and Home Assistant all let the customer's box do the work, and all still run central rendezvous plus a relay fallback; Home Assistant's remote access, now called Home Assistant Link, costs US$6.50 a month ([Nabu Casa](https://www.nabucasa.com/link/)). An ESP32-S3 could fan out voice to nine peers easily, but it sits behind the same router and carrier NAT as everything else, and asking a grandparent to enable UPnP collides with standard security advice. A Raspberry Pi hub adds an estimated NZ$70 to 90 per family and a Linux image to patch for years without fixing reachability. The realistic self-host unit is one "family tech person" running the published relay for several households.

Managed platforms do remove the server. A standard TURN relay accepts the current UDP packets with a thin client wrapper; media platforms such as an SFU or LiveKit need WebRTC. Cloudflare charges US$0.05 per GB for its media relay and TURN together, after a shared 1,000 GB monthly allowance ([Cloudflare pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/sfu/platform/pricing/); the conflict noted earlier is resolved). LiveKit's ESP32 SDK no longer carries a developer-preview warning but supports only the S3 and P4 and one subscribed audio track ([LiveKit docs](https://docs.livekit.io/frontends/build/hardware/esp32/)); its Scale plan is US$500 a month with 1.5 million participant-minutes included, then US$0.0004 a minute, and caps concurrent connections at 5,000 ([LiveKit pricing](https://livekit.io/pricing.md)), well below a Christmas-morning peak at 100,000 families. Per-message brokers such as AWS IoT Core suit presence but not audio. The researcher's cost estimates, on an assumed 20 minutes of talk a day per four-unit family at 46 kbit/s, are below.

| Families | Cloudflare media relay, voice only | Cloudflare Durable Objects, voice and presence | LiveKit Scale, connect only to talk | iroh Pro, units only | Self-run VPS relays |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | US$0 | US$71 to 129 | US$860 | US$19 | about US$6 to 11 |
| 10,000 | US$261 | US$715 to 1,291 | US$9,821 | US$109 | about US$22 to 39 |
| 100,000 | US$3,055 | US$7,155 to 12,915 | US$101,810 | US$1,189 | about US$220 to 390 |

*Monthly, researcher's arithmetic. The Cloudflare media relay and LiveKit columns were re-checked against list prices read on 15 November 2026 and match. The Durable Objects prices were confirmed (US$0.15 per million requests, US$12.50 per million GB-seconds, incoming WebSocket messages billed at 20 to 1) but that column's arithmetic was not redone. The iroh column counts every unit as an endpoint and excludes relayed traffic at US$0.09/GB, which applies only to pairs that cannot connect directly. VPS plan sizes were assumed, not sourced.*

Money does not separate the options. Several of these vendors changed their pricing or terms in the last two years, so the device protocol should stay provider-neutral with relay endpoints in signed, updatable settings.

## People and continuity are the burden, not bandwidth

Every comparable consumer voice device the research found runs on a vendor-operated service, almost all funded by subscription; none was peer-to-peer only. A device sold once with a free-forever relay has no precedent among them. Signal's published costs show where the money goes: about US$19 million a year on roughly 50 staff against US$2.8 million on bandwidth, of which calls take 20 petabytes and US$1.7 million, as of November 2023 ([Signal](https://signal.org/blog/signal-is-expensive/)). Its phone-number verification costs more than its bandwidth, about US$6 million, a reminder that onboarding is a cost line too. The closest analogue, the Tin Can kids' Wi-Fi phone, went down on Christmas morning 2025 when call volume jumped a hundredfold from the day before, with outages across the US ([CPR News, Feb 2026](https://www.cpr.org/2026/02/04/tin-can-kids-landline-cellphone-alternative/)); its own January status pages have since been removed. That is the failure to plan for: every unit gifted on 25 December activates at once, and that spike hits rendezvous and onboarding in a peer-to-peer design just as it hits a relay.

The stronger commercial risk is the device dying with its vendor. Insteon's servers went dark without notice in April 2022; Home Assistant warned owners never to factory reset, though reports conflicted on whether a reset hub could be recovered ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2022/04/19/insteon_cloud_shutdown/)). Moxie's rescue server worked only for robots that had taken an enabling update before shutdown ([AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/24/12/20/moxie-robot-may-be-saved-by-a-last-minute-open-sourcing-effort)). The escape hatch must ship in the firmware. Under the Consumer Guarantees Act goods must be of acceptable quality, which includes durable ([Consumer Protection NZ](https://consumerprotection.govt.nz/general-help/consumer-laws/consumer-guarantees-act)); the researcher infers that a radio bricked by a relay shutdown is plausibly a durability failure, though no NZ authority on that point was found.

On interception law, the statute was read first-hand this time and changes the earlier reading. The Telecommunications (Interception Capability and Security) Act defines a service provider as anyone, inside or outside New Zealand, who provides or makes available in New Zealand a telecommunications service to an end-user, business or not, and defines a telecommunications service as any goods, services, equipment or facilities that enable or facilitate telecommunication ([TICSA s 3](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2013/0091/latest/whole.html)). On a literal reading, selling the radios alone makes the vendor a service provider, so a hardware-only model does not clearly sit outside the Act (corrected). A service provider need not register but is encouraged to ([NZ Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/advice-services/businesses-and-organisations/telecommunications-interception-capability-security-act)), and must give reasonable technical help to an agency holding a warrant, including decrypting where it provided the encryption; the Act says it need not ensure an agency can decrypt (s 24). Because keys never leave the units, help would be limited to metadata and ciphertext in every architecture. Running a relay adds a smaller risk of being a "network operator", defined to include anyone operating a data network used by the public; network operators must register and carry heavier duties, reduced below 4,000 customers. Radio compliance applies identically to every architecture ([RSM compliance guide](https://www.rsm.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/documents/compliance-guide.pdf)).

## Recommendation and next steps

Do not switch to Meshtastic, and do not rewrite Stages 1 and 2. Keep the blind relay, because it is the lowest-risk design and its cost is trivial, but change what the owner is committed to maintaining. The target for a commercial version is a layered design: units on the same network talk directly; units in different homes punch a direct path using free address discovery and a sealed rendezvous record; pairs that cannot connect fall back to a relay chosen from a list in signed settings, which can be a standard TURN service such as Cloudflare's, a family-hosted copy of the published relay, or the owner's own. iroh is a credible alternative that bundles the direct path and the fallback for US$19 a month, at the cost of moving to QUIC. Updates become signed static files, and offline alerts a scheduled function. That leaves nothing to patch, shrinks the service the owner provides to rendezvous and alerts, and lets the product outlive its vendor. It does not remove support calls, the update channel's risk, Christmas-morning spikes, or interception-law duties.

The first step costs almost nothing and should go into the protocol spec now: store the relay address and key as a list in changeable settings and never compile them in, identify units by key pair, and make sure pairing and factory reset never need the owner's servers. Use libpeer, not esp_peer, in anything that is not an Espressif chip. Next, measure instead of estimating: filter the published libp2p dataset for New Zealand networks, then run a two-server STUN check from every candidate family home and as many NZ providers as can be reached, to replace the 75 to 95% figure with data. Then prototype hole punching, a TURN fallback carrying the existing sealed packets, and arbiter-free floor control in the planned laptop test client before any firmware work. Before selling, get a short legal opinion on whether the vendor is a service provider or network operator under the Act, ask the Police registrar directly, confirm the radio compliance route, and settle any Tailscale or iroh terms in writing. Publish the relay with a one-command deploy and a written end-of-service commitment, and load-test a mass reconnect.

## Conclusion

The question assumed the relay was the problem. The evidence points elsewhere: a relay that only forwards sealed packets is already the design that Tailscale, Plex, Synology and Home Assistant converged on, and its running cost rounds to nothing. Removing it does not remove the vendor's legal duties either. What threatens a commercial product is a single operator being the only thing between a grandparent and silence, whether that operator runs a relay, a rendezvous service or an update server.

So the valuable property is replaceability, with decentralisation as one way to get it. A radio that can be re-pointed by one setting, that pairs without its maker, and that talks directly when the network allows has no single point whose failure ends the product. Whether the direct path is worth building at all turns on one unknown this research could not resolve: how NZ carrier-grade NAT actually behaves, which a weekend of testing at real homes will answer.
