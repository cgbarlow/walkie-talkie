# Shrink the relay; you cannot delete it

No option removes every central piece, but the owner can stop maintaining a relay fleet. Meshtastic and other licence-free radio cannot carry live, clear, encrypted voice between towns, so they are out as a replacement. Direct peer-to-peer over home Wi-Fi is feasible on the ESP32-S3 and would connect most New Zealand home pairs (a researcher's estimate of 75 to 95%, not a measurement), but the rest need a relay of last resort, and every design still needs rendezvous, firmware updates and something always-on to send offline alerts. The research also shows the relay's bandwidth is cheap at any scale (cents per family per month); the real burden is on-call availability, activation spikes, the update channel and support, and peer-to-peer removes little of that. The recommended path is to keep the blind relay for Stages 1 and 2, make it replaceable from day one, then add a direct path with a pay-per-use fallback so that a commercial version runs on static files and serverless functions with no server to patch. On the notes' reading, a hardware-only vendor sits outside New Zealand's interception law while a relay operator is probably a "service provider" under it; that needs a formal opinion before selling.

> **How far to trust this report.** All five researchers reported that most source pages could not be opened in this session. Their cited findings rest on search-result extracts of the linked pages, not full reads, and vendor prices and statute wording were not checked first-hand. Several figures are the researchers' own estimates or arithmetic; they are labelled as estimates below and none is a measurement. Nothing here is legal advice.

## Radio mesh cannot carry Grandad's voice to the next town

Meshtastic is a text mesh. Its default LongFast preset runs at about **1.07 kbit/s** ([Meshtastic blog](https://meshtastic.org/blog/why-your-mesh-should-switch-from-longfast/)), which is below even the lowest practical voice codec once headers are counted, and its only audio feature is an experimental Codec2 module documented for 2.4 GHz SX128x radios ([Meshtastic audio docs](https://meshtastic.org/docs/configuration/module/audio/)). A third-party project, Voicetastic, sends whole voice clips with error recovery, defaulting to Codec2 at 1.2 kbit/s ([Voicetastic wiki](https://github.com/voicetastic/voicetastic-core/wiki/Voice-Protocol)). The researcher computed that a 10-second clip would take roughly 20 to 30 seconds to cross a single hop on LongFast, and more per extra hop; that is the researcher's calculation from Voicetastic's published packet sizes and pacing, since no measured latency was found. Codec2 at these rates is a robotic-sounding vocoder, a real risk for a listener with hearing loss, against the project's 16 to 24 kbit/s Opus. No study of Codec2 for hearing-impaired listeners was found.

Riding the community mesh is not an answer either. Wellington's mesh reported more than 150 nodes and was "becoming unusably congested" ([Meshtastic forum](https://meshtastic.discourse.group/t/wellington-region-new-zealand/10171)), and Gisborne District Council hit "technical limitations" with Meshtastic before adopting MeshCore as one of six backup modes ([GDC](https://www.gdc.govt.nz/council/news/media-releases/back-up-comms-crucial-in-emergencies)). These are volunteer text networks built on hilltop repeaters; coverage outside a few regions cannot be promised to buyers.

The alternatives fail on at least one requirement each. Reticulum's LXST offers real-time voice over LoRa, but its ecosystem reports it only over a single hop ([reticulum-meshchat](https://github.com/markqvist/reticulum-meshchat)). Purpose-built ESP32 plus SX1262 walkie-talkies exist, such as LILYGO's T3-S3 MVSR at Codec2 3200 bps ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2025/03/28/a-lora-based-walkie-talkie-meet-lilygo-t3-s3-mvsr-lora-voice-communication-kit/)), but reach a street or suburb, not the next town. Amateur radio is ruled out because transmissions "must not be encoded for the purpose of obscuring their meaning" ([ZL2GT summary](https://zl2gt.nz/radio-regulations-summary/)). No source lists New Zealand among Amazon Sidewalk countries ([Ring](https://ring.com/support/articles/2a1ku/Amazon-Sidewalk-Information)). Cellular (LTE-M, Cat-1) removes the home Wi-Fi dependence but swaps the relay for a carrier and still needs a server.

Legally, 915 to 928 MHz is usable licence-free at up to 1 W EIRP under the Short Range Devices licence, with strict out-of-band limits that the regulator is actively reminding LoRa users about ([Gazette 2022-go3100](https://gazette.govt.nz/notice/id/2022-go3100); [RSM](https://www.rsm.govt.nz/about/news-and-updates/lorawan-and-compliance-with-general-user-radio-licence-gurl-conditions)). The researcher could not read the current licence table first-hand, and found no primary source on whether encryption is permitted on these bands. Adding a LoRa radio also adds a second transmitter to declare and test for the finished product ([Compliance Notice 2020](https://gazette.govt.nz/notice/id/2021-go52)). Radio is credible only as an optional fallback: ESP-NOW between units on the same property costs nothing in hardware, and an optional LoRa add-on could carry the tiny offline and low-battery alerts.

## Direct peer-to-peer reaches most NZ homes, never all

The ESP32-S3 can do this. Espressif's `esp_peer` provides ICE, STUN and TURN with Opus audio in under 60 KB of RAM in a minimal setup ([esp_peer README](https://components.espressif.com/components/espressif/esp_peer/versions/1.5.8/readme)), and the MIT-licensed libpeer it derives from is available ([libpeer](https://github.com/sepfy/libpeer)). The researcher's view is that the lowest-risk route keeps the existing sealed-Opus-over-UDP packets and adds only a small hole-punching layer, because the project already has its own encryption.

The obstacle is New Zealand's broadband mix. **2degrees moves every dynamic-IP home connection behind carrier-grade NAT**, with a static address at NZ$10 a month ([2degrees](https://2degrees.nz/help/broadband-help/ip-addresses/2degrees-ip-addresses)). Fixed wireless is about 20% of connections and Starlink about 3% nationally and 19% of rural connections ([TCF Annual Report 2025](https://www.tcf.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/TCF-Annual-Report-2025-Final-1.pdf)), and both sit behind carrier-grade NAT by default; Starlink's own router allows no inbound traffic or port forwarding ([Starlink support](https://starlink.com/support/article/1192f3ef-2a17-31d9-261a-a59d215629f4)). IPv6 will rescue few pairs: APNIC's figures put Spark at 0.6% IPv6-capable, 2degrees at about 46% and One NZ at about 39% ([APNIC](https://www.apnic.net/community/ipv6/ipv6-in-your-region/?cc=NZ)). Spark fibre and One NZ fibre NAT policy in 2026 could not be confirmed.

Carrier-grade NAT does not by itself block hole punching; what matters is how the outer NAT assigns ports. Measured success elsewhere brackets the answer: **about 70%** in a study of 4.4 million libp2p attempts ([arXiv 2510.27500](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27500v1)) and "well north of 90%" in Tailscale's own metrics, which still fall back to a relay ([Tailscale](https://tailscale.com/blog/nat-traversal-improvements-pt-1)). The researcher's estimate for NZ home pairs is **75 to 95% direct**, built by multiplying figures from different sources; no measurement of NAT behaviour on any NZ network was found. A separate researcher estimated that only 35 to 55% of NZ homes could accept an inbound connection with no customer action, which is why "one unit in the family acts as the server" cannot be the only path. Rest homes, hospitals and guest Wi-Fi that block UDP matter for this audience too.

Three small services survive the move to peer-to-peer. Address discovery (STUN) is available free; Cloudflare's is unmetered ([Cloudflare TURN FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/turn/faq/)). Rendezvous, the way two units learn each other's current address, can be a stateless mailbox of sealed blobs: far smaller than a media relay, and a compromise reveals only IP addresses and timing. The relay for pairs that cannot punch through is the part that cannot vanish. The choices are to screen homes at set-up and accept failures, let a well-connected family unit forward for the others (which does nothing for a two-unit pair that both sit behind hard NAT), or buy fallback per gigabyte.

Groups work without a server. The 3GPP off-network floor-control procedure is a ready model: request the floor, wait briefly for objection, then announce "floor taken" ([TS 24.380 A.4](https://itecspec.com/spec/3gpp-24-380-a-4-off-network-floor-control-signalling-flows)). A ten-unit group has 45 pairwise paths, so the mesh must tolerate missing links by forwarding through a member both sides can reach.

Security shifts instead of improving. Each member's home IP address becomes visible to the others, and the convenient central point for re-keying and dropping a lost unit disappears; the replacement is a membership list signed by an owner unit and passed between units, which is more firmware to write. The worst consumer incident in this class came from a peer-to-peer platform, not a relay: ThroughTek's Kalay let an attacker who knew a device ID register as that device and reach live audio and video, on a platform claiming 83 million devices ([Mandiant](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/mandiant-discloses-critical-vulnerability-affecting-iot-devices)). The lesson applies to any rendezvous service: units must prove identity with their key pair, not an ID. One researcher's conclusion was blunt, and it is the fair one: for this threat model a hardened blind relay is the lower-risk design, and peer-to-peer is a resilience and cost optimisation.

## Borrowed networks and customer boxes move the burden

Riding someone else's network sounds like the escape, and the research found no shipped consumer voice device that does it. iroh is the only peer-to-peer stack with a vendor-published ESP32 port, but its free public relays are for "development, testing, and hobby projects" with no uptime guarantee, and production use means a paid plan or self-hosting ([iroh public relays](https://docs.iroh.computer/iroh-services/relays/public)). An unofficial MIT-licensed Tailscale client for the ESP32-S3 exists and reports 30 to 150 ms round trips through Tailscale's relays, but it takes 15 to 20 seconds from boot to connected and is "not affiliated with or endorsed by Tailscale Inc." ([MicroLink](https://github.com/CamM2325/microlink)); Tailscale's terms for embedding in a sold device could not be read. ZeroTier requires a commercial licence for devices built on it ([ZeroTier forum](https://discuss.zerotier.com/t/licensing-and-commercial-use/8310)). Tor adds seconds of delay. Public MQTT brokers say plainly they are "not intended for private or production data" ([HiveMQ](https://www.hivemq.com/public-mqtt-broker/)). Free shared back ends also change terms once load grows: matrix.org added fair-use limits in 2025 ([Matrix](https://matrix.org/blog/2025/07/terms-update/)) and Meshtastic restricted its public MQTT server in 2024 ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Enhancements-coming-soon-to-Meshtastic-s-public-server.862237.0.html)). A fleet that only consumes volunteers' bandwidth is the pattern that gets rate-limited. The useful finds are narrow: Nostr relays and the BitTorrent DHT could serve as a rendezvous channel nobody owns, though no ready ESP32 DHT client was found.

Customer hosting has the same shape. Tailscale, Plex, Synology and Home Assistant's Nabu Casa all let the customer's box do the work, and all still run central rendezvous plus a relay fallback; Nabu Casa charges EUR 7.50 a month for it ([Nabu Casa](https://support.nabucasa.com/hc/en-us/sections/26496346499997-Home-Assistant-remote-access)). An ESP32-S3 could fan out voice to nine peers easily, but it sits behind the same router and carrier NAT as everything else, and asking a grandparent to enable UPnP collides with standard security advice. A Raspberry Pi hub adds an estimated NZ$70 to 90 per family and a Linux image to patch for years without fixing reachability. The realistic self-host unit is one "family tech person" running the published relay for several households.

Managed platforms do remove the server. None accepts the current raw UDP protocol, so the firmware would move to WebRTC or WebSocket. Cloudflare's media relay is priced at US$0.05 per GB after a 1,000 GB monthly allowance ([Cloudflare pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/sfu/platform/pricing/)), though one Cloudflare overview page says TURN alone has no free tier, so that needs re-reading. LiveKit's ESP32 SDK is a developer preview "not ready for production" ([LiveKit docs](https://docs.livekit.io/frontends/build/hardware/esp32/)), and per-message brokers such as AWS IoT Core suit presence but not audio. The researcher's cost estimates, on an assumed 20 minutes of talk a day per four-unit family, are below.

| Families | Cloudflare media relay, voice only | Cloudflare Durable Objects, voice and presence | LiveKit, connect only to talk | Self-run VPS relays |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | US$0 | US$71 to 129 | US$860 | about US$6 to 11 |
| 10,000 | US$261 | US$715 to 1,291 | US$9,821 | about US$22 to 39 |
| 100,000 | US$3,055 | US$7,155 to 12,915 | US$101,810 | about US$220 to 390 |

*Monthly, researcher's arithmetic from list prices seen in search extracts on 14 November 2026; VPS plan sizes were assumed, not sourced.*

Money does not separate the options. Three of these vendors changed their pricing model in the last two years, so the device protocol should stay provider-neutral with relay endpoints in signed, updatable settings.

## People and continuity are the burden, not bandwidth

Every comparable consumer voice device the research found runs on a vendor-operated service, almost all funded by subscription; none was peer-to-peer only. A device sold once with a free-forever relay has no precedent among them. Signal's published costs show where the money goes: about US$19 million a year on staff against US$2.8 million on bandwidth ([Signal](https://signal.org/blog/signal-is-expensive/)). The closest analogue, the Tin Can kids' Wi-Fi phone, went down on Christmas morning 2025 when call volume rose more than 100 times, and was still "not fully stable" on 7 January ([Tin Can](https://tincan.kids/pages/service-update-jan-7-2026)). That is the failure to plan for: every unit gifted on 25 December activates at once, and that spike hits rendezvous and onboarding in a peer-to-peer design just as it hits a relay.

The stronger commercial risk is the device dying with its vendor. Insteon's servers vanished without notice in April 2022; directly linked devices kept working but a factory reset was fatal because set-up needed the servers ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2022/04/19/insteon_cloud_shutdown/)). Moxie's rescue server worked only for robots that had taken an enabling update before shutdown ([AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/24/12/20/moxie-robot-may-be-saved-by-a-last-minute-open-sourcing-effort)). The escape hatch must ship in the firmware. Under the Consumer Guarantees Act goods must last a reasonable time ([Consumer Protection NZ](https://consumerprotection.govt.nz/general-help/consumer-laws/consumer-guarantees-act)); the researcher infers that a radio bricked by a relay shutdown is plausibly a durability failure, though no NZ authority on that point was found.

On interception law, the Telecommunications (Interception Capability and Security) Act defines a "service provider" as anyone who provides a telecommunications service to an end-user in New Zealand; service providers need not register but are encouraged to ([NZ Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/advice-services/businesses-and-organisations/telecommunications-interception-capability-security-act)). The researcher's reading, with the statute not read in full, is that a relay operator is probably a service provider with a duty to assist under warrant, and arguably but uncertainly a "network operator" with heavier duties; because keys never leave the devices, assistance would be limited to metadata and ciphertext. A vendor selling only hardware, with families hosting or audio flowing direct, appears to fall outside both definitions, though a vendor-run rendezvous or alert service is still a thinner service. Radio compliance (a Supplier's Declaration of Conformity and labelling) applies identically to every architecture ([RSM compliance guide](https://www.rsm.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/documents/compliance-guide.pdf)).

## The options side by side

| Option | Owner still runs or pays for | Works for NZ homes | Cost | Security | Legal position |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Meshtastic or other LoRa | Nothing central; families would need repeaters | No: not live, poor audio, suburb range | Extra radio per unit (not priced) | Fine on LoRa; none on PRS or amateur | Second transmitter to certify |
| Pure peer-to-peer, no fallback | Rendezvous, updates, alerts | Est. 75 to 95% of pairs; the rest never connect | Near zero | Home IPs visible to members; no central revoke | Closest to hardware-only |
| Peer-to-peer plus paid fallback | Rendezvous, credential function, updates, alerts, all serverless | All homes that allow UDP | Cents per family per month (est.) | As above; fallback sees only ciphertext | Thin service remains |
| Borrowed overlay (iroh, Tailscale) | Paid plan or commercial deal; updates | Good | Unverified | Depends on a third party's control plane | Terms unverified |
| Customer or family-hosted relay | Published relay software; updates | Est. 35 to 55% of homes can host unaided; a VPS always can | NZ$0 to 9 a month per host | Blind relay; exposes a home if hosted there | Vendor appears outside interception law |
| Managed platform as the relay | Platform code, account, updates, alerts | All homes | US$0.03 to 0.13 per family per month (est.) | Blind if units encrypt first | Still a service provider |
| Current self-run blind relay | A small server, on-call | All homes | Under US$0.01 per family per month (est.) | Lowest-risk model; metadata only | Probably a service provider |

## Recommendation and next steps

Do not switch to Meshtastic, and do not rewrite Stages 1 and 2. Keep the blind relay, because it is the lowest-risk design and its cost is trivial, but change what the owner is committed to maintaining. The target for a commercial version is a layered design: units on the same network talk directly; units in different homes punch a direct path using free address discovery and a sealed rendezvous record; pairs that cannot connect fall back to a relay chosen from a list in signed settings, which can be a pay-per-use managed relay, a family-hosted copy of the published relay, or the owner's own. Updates become signed static files, and offline alerts a scheduled function. That leaves nothing to patch, shrinks the service the owner provides to rendezvous and alerts, and lets the product outlive its vendor. It does not remove support calls, the update channel's risk, or Christmas-morning spikes.

The first step costs almost nothing and should go into the protocol spec now: store the relay address and key as a list in changeable settings and never compile them in, identify units by key pair, and make sure pairing and factory reset never need the owner's servers. Next, measure instead of estimating: run a two-server STUN check from every candidate family home, and as many NZ providers as can be reached, to replace the 75 to 95% figure with data. Then prototype hole punching and arbiter-free floor control in the planned laptop test client before any firmware work. Before a business case, re-read the vendor price and terms pages first-hand, particularly Cloudflare's free allowance and whether its relay may be used outside browsers. Before selling, get a short legal opinion on interception-law status and ask the Police registrar directly, and confirm the radio compliance route. Publish the relay with a one-command deploy and a written end-of-service commitment, and load-test a mass reconnect.

## Conclusion

The question assumed the relay was the problem. The evidence points elsewhere: a relay that only forwards sealed packets is already the design that Tailscale, Plex, Synology and Nabu Casa converged on, and its running cost rounds to nothing. What threatens a commercial product is a single operator being the only thing between a grandparent and silence, whether that operator runs a relay, a rendezvous service or an update server.

So the valuable property is replaceability, with decentralisation as one way to get it. A radio that can be re-pointed by one setting, that pairs without its maker, and that talks directly when the network allows has no single point whose failure ends the product. Whether the direct path is worth building at all turns on one unknown this research could not resolve: how NZ carrier-grade NAT actually behaves, which a weekend of testing at real homes will answer.
