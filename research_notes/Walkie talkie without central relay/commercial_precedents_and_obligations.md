## 1. Precedent products: architecture and business model

### Takeaway
Every comparable consumer voice device found runs on a vendor-operated central service and almost all fund it with a subscription (roughly US$7-15/month for kids' devices, US$10-40+/month for elder devices); none of those found is P2P-only. The closest 2025-26 analogue, Tin Can, shows the realistic failure mode: not bandwidth cost but a demand spike (100x on Christmas Day) knocking out a centrally hosted calling service for weeks.

### Cited Findings
- Tin Can (kids' Wi-Fi "landline", device about US$100): service crashed Christmas morning 2025 after a surge of new users; founder Chet Kittleson said Christmas Day call volume rose more than 100x versus the start of the month; many devices could not make or receive calls — [Business Insider via AOL](https://www.aol.com/articles/tin-landline-kids-become-popular-203939896.html); [The Star, 27 Apr 2026](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/04/27/the-hottest-phone-for-kids-right-now-is-a-us100-landline)
- Tin Can was still "not fully stable yet" on 7 Jan 2026 (company service update signed by the three co-founders, saying stabilising was "our only priority") — i.e. about two weeks of degraded service — [Tin Can service update, 7 Jan 2026](https://tincan.kids/pages/service-update-jan-7-2026)
- Tin Can reportedly apologised and waived its monthly fee after the outage (mirror of the Business Insider story; not verified against the original) — [mirror](https://thenote.app/post/en/tin-can-landline-for-kids-is-so-popular-it-crashed-at-christmas-bveeko3q0k?amp=true)
- Tin Can pricing: Tin Can-to-Tin Can calls free; paid "Party Line" plan to call outside numbers reported at US$9.99/month (US$99.99/year) by most reviews, and US$14.99/month (US$149.99/year) by another source — sources conflict, possibly a price rise — [Domino review](https://domino.com/style-shopping/tin-can-kids-landline-phone-honest-review); [pausegadget comparison](https://pausegadget.com/tin-can-vs-waffle/); [White Hatter review](https://www.thewhitehatter.ca/post/tincan-kids-phone-white-hatter-review)
- Relay (Republic Wireless screenless LTE/Wi-Fi kids' walkie-talkie, 2018): launched at US$99.99 for one / US$199.99 for three plus US$6.99/month per device; later retail US$49.99 device plus US$9.99/month, no contract — central-carrier model with per-device subscription — [BestMVNO](https://bestmvno.com/republic-wireless/republic-wireless-announces-relay); [Hip2Save 2019](https://hip2save.com/2019/12/15/20-off-relay-phone-for-kids-gps-tracking-2-way-communication-no-contract/); [Techlicious](https://techlicious.com/blog/republic-wireless-relay-screen-free-phone)
- Apple Watch Walkie-Talkie is a FaceTime Audio call with push-to-talk gating (so it uses Apple's central FaceTime signalling/relay infrastructure); first session set-up took 5-10 s in the WWDC 2018 demo, then near-instant — [AppleInsider, 5 Jun 2018](https://appleinsider.com/articles/18/06/05/apple-watchs-walkie-talkie-feature-in-watchos-5-uses-facetime-audio-technology)
- One 2026 report says Walkie-Talkie disappeared in the watchOS 27 beta, while Apple support pages still document it — conflicting, treat as unconfirmed — [WebProNews](https://www.webpronews.com/apple-watch-loses-its-voice-walkie-talkie-vanishes-in-watchos-27-beta/); [Apple support](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/HT208917)
- GrandPad (elder tablet): device about US$349 on the official site in one review; service about US$40/month (US$38 AARP) including 4G and 24/7 support in one review, US$59 in an older one — sources conflict by date/seller — [SeniorLiving.org 2026](https://www.seniorliving.org/cell-phone/consumer-cellular/grandpad/); [GrandPad buy page](https://www.grandpad.net/buy)
- Komp (No Isolation, one-knob elder video screen): 2022 help page gives GBP 699 inc. VAT plus Komp Family subscription GBP 19/month; subscription mandatory for units ordered after 28 Nov 2022; covers 4G data and support (likely outdated price) — [Komp help centre](https://help.komp.family/kompnov22); [Komp subscription](https://help.komp.family/knowledge/komp-subscription)
- ViewClix (elder video frame): Premier Membership US$9.95/month, one membership per frame shared by the whole family — [ViewClix Premier](https://viewclix.com/premier)

### Inferences
- The market norm for "appliance" communication devices is hardware at or near cost plus a recurring fee that pays for the service; the subscription, not the hardware margin, is what keeps the relay alive. A NZ$35-50 BOM device sold once with a free-forever relay has no precedent among the products found.
- Tin Can's incident is the most relevant single data point: the bottleneck was a sudden step in concurrent users (gift-day activation), which is an onboarding/capacity-planning problem that also exists for a blind relay (all devices activate on 25 December).
- Elder-market products (GrandPad, Komp) charge the most and bundle human support; this suggests support for non-technical users, not relay compute, is the dominant recurring cost in this segment.

### Gaps
- No reliable architecture/pricing found in this session for Karri, Bond Touch, Toymail, Yoto/Toniebox, Xplora/Gabb/Cosmo, Zello, Loop, Nucleus, Echo Drop-In. UNVERIFIED background knowledge: Zello is a central store-and-forward relay (free consumer app, paid Zello Work); Echo Drop-In uses Amazon's cloud; Gabb/Cosmo/Xplora watches are cellular with roughly US$10-25/month plans; Yoto/Tonies are content-cloud devices with offline playback of already-downloaded cards; Bond Touch and Karri are cloud-relayed. None of these should be cited without checking.
- Tin Can's technical architecture (SIP provider, own switching) and funding/unit numbers were not found.
- Relay (Relay Inc) current consumer status not confirmed; UNVERIFIED recollection is that it pivoted to an enterprise product (RelayPro/Relay+).

## 2. Bricked-by-shutdown cautionary tales and end-of-life regulation

### Takeaway
Cloud-dependent devices routinely die with their vendor, sometimes with zero notice (Insteon) and sometimes within a year of sale (Humane); the only soft landings came where local operation or a releasable local server existed (Insteon local links, OpenMoxie). Regulators are moving toward mandatory disclosure of support periods (FTC staff view 2024, UK PSTI 2024, EU CRA 2026-27), but NZ has no connected-product-specific rule — only the general Consumer Guarantees Act durability guarantee.

### Cited Findings
- Insteon: cloud servers went offline about 14-15 Apr 2022 without notice; hubs stopped working; staff LinkedIn profiles showed departures; directly linked switches/scenes kept working locally, but new devices could not be added and factory reset was fatal because first-time set-up required the servers — [The Register, 19 Apr 2022](https://www.theregister.com/2022/04/19/insteon_cloud_shutdown/); [AppleInsider, 18 Apr 2022](https://appleinsider.com/articles/22/04/18/smarthome-firm-and-early-homekit-partner-insteon-is-dead-with-no-warning-to-customers); [TinkerTry](https://tinkertry.com/insteon-turns-off-cloud-but-local-controls-still-work)
- Insteon was reportedly bought by a group of users (June 2022) and services restored (single-source, Wikipedia) — [Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/INSTEON)
- Humane AI Pin (US$700): announced 18 Feb 2025, servers off 28 Feb 2025 (10 days' notice); calling, messaging and AI stopped; refunds only for units shipped on/after 15 Nov 2024; HP bought the assets for US$116m but not the Pin business — [MacRumors, 18 Feb 2025](https://www.macrumors.com/2025/02/18/humane-ai-pin-discontinued/); [Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/ai/the-humane-ai-pin-is-officially-dead-and-hp-is-buying-the-companys-assets-for-116-million); [SlashGear](https://www.slashgear.com/1794149/humane-ai-pin-shutdown-timeline-refund-details)
- Spotify Car Thing: announced 23 May 2024, disabled 9 Dec 2024; refund position conflicting (initially none, later case-by-case after legal threats) — [9to5Google, 9 Dec 2024](https://9to5google.com/2024/12/09/spotify-officially-kills-car-thing-remaining-units-no-longer-work/); [Spotify support](https://support.spotify.com/article/car-thing-discontinued/)
- Moxie (Embodied, about US$800 children's robot): cloud shut down 30 Jan 2025; robot was wholly cloud-dependent. Former engineers produced OpenMoxie, a local server run on a home computer, but it only works for units that installed OTA update 24.10.803 before the servers went dark; Embodied gives no ongoing support; community-maintained — [AppleInsider, 20 Dec 2024](https://appleinsider.com/articles/24/12/20/moxie-robot-may-be-saved-by-a-last-minute-open-sourcing-effort); [Fight to Repair](https://fighttorepair.substack.com/p/moxie-libre-push-to-open-source-may); [Robots Around the House forum](https://robotsaroundthehouse.com/threads/moxie-christmas-miracle-embodied-announce-openmoxie-solution.767/latest)
- Open Home Foundation's framing: "All cloud-based products will come to an end" — [Open Home Foundation newsletter](https://newsletter.openhomefoundation.org/all-cloud-based-products-will-come-to-an-end/)
- FTC staff paper, 26 Nov 2024: of 184 connected products surveyed, 161 (FTC press release figure; ~89%) did not disclose how long software support would last; staff say failure to disclose may violate the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act for products with written warranties and may be deceptive/unfair under FTC Act s 5 (staff views, not binding rules) — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/11/smart-products-surveyed-fail-provide-consumers-information-how-long-companies-will-provide-software); [FTC staff paper PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/smart-device-makers-failure-to-provide-software-updates-may-leave-you-smarting.pdf)
- EU Cyber Resilience Act: Article 14 vulnerability/incident reporting applies from 11 Sep 2026 (including to products already on the market, and continuing after the support period ends); main product-security obligations apply December 2027; manufacturers must provide security updates for the expected product lifetime and at least five years unless the intended lifetime is shorter — [Travers Smith](https://www.traverssmith.com/knowledge/knowledge-container/the-eu-cyber-resilience-acts-vulnerability-and-incident-reporting-requirements-are-now-live/); [Matheson](https://www.matheson.com/insights/the-eu-cyber-resilience-act-recap-reporting-obligations/); [Irish NCSC CRA page](https://ncsc.gov.ie/cra/)
- NZ Consumer Guarantees Act: goods must be of acceptable quality, which includes being durable / lasting a reasonable time, judged by what a reasonable consumer would regard as acceptable; CGA rights continue after any manufacturer warranty expires; spare parts/repair facilities must be available for a reasonable time — [Consumer Protection NZ](https://consumerprotection.govt.nz/general-help/consumer-laws/consumer-guarantees-act); [Consumer NZ](https://www.consumer.org.nz/articles/consumer-guarantees-act)
- A NZ dispute over smartwatch software upgrades went against retailer Noel Leeming, with the reporting stressing that tech products must "last the distance" under the CGA — [NZ Herald](https://nzherald.co.nz/business/apple-watch-stoush-noel-leeming-loses-case-over-smartwatch-upgrades/FM2HEZZPVXLQ6ZVXIPUH25WIHA)

### Inferences
- Under the CGA, a commercially sold radio that stops working because the vendor turned off the relay after, say, 18 months is plausibly a durability failure against the supplier (and manufacturer), with refund/replacement remedies. This is an inference from the general guarantee; no NZ authority found addresses service shutdown of a connected product specifically. Clear point-of-sale disclosure ("works with our relay until at least <date>; can be re-pointed to your own relay") shifts what a "reasonable consumer" would expect, but the CGA cannot be contracted out of for consumer sales.
- The Moxie lesson is specific and actionable: the escape hatch must already be in the firmware shipped (configurable server address, key material not held only by the vendor) because a post-mortem OTA reaches only devices that happen to be online in the final weeks.
- The Insteon lesson: set-up/onboarding must not require the vendor's servers, otherwise a factory reset permanently bricks the unit.
- If the product were ever sold into the UK/EU, PSTI and CRA obligations (declared support period, vulnerability reporting, 5-year updates) would apply regardless of whether a relay exists; they attach to the product with digital elements, not to the relay.

### Gaps
- Revolv (2016), Jibo, Nucleus, Toymail, Mycroft, Amazon Halo (2023), Wink's 2020 subscription pivot: not verified this session. UNVERIFIED background: Nest bricked Revolv hubs in May 2016; Amazon Halo ended 31 Jul 2023 with refunds; Wink announced a US$4.99/month mandatory subscription with a week's notice in May 2020; Mycroft wound down in 2023 with community continuation (OpenVoiceOS/Neon). A Home Assistant forum comment calls Nucleus "another half paperweight" but gives no dates — [forum](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/hey-insteon-users/413461?page=2).
- UK PSTI Act: the search did not confirm details. UNVERIFIED background: regime in force 29 Apr 2024; bans universal default passwords, requires a vulnerability-disclosure contact and a published minimum security-update period. Check OPSS guidance.
- No NZ equivalent of PSTI/CRA was found; whether NZ plans one was not researched. Fair Trading Act (misleading representations about "lifetime" service) was not researched.

## 3. Realistic size of the relay burden

### Takeaway
Bandwidth and compute for a blind Opus relay are small: by my arithmetic about 2 TB/month at 10,000 families and about 21 TB/month at 100,000 (per listener), i.e. tens to low thousands of dollars a month; Signal's published figures confirm relay bandwidth is a minor line next to people. The real burden is human: availability/on-call, onboarding spikes, firmware-update safety, abuse/DoS handling and customer support.

### Cited Findings
- Signal ("Privacy is Priceless, but Signal is Expensive", 16 Nov 2023): infrastructure about US$14m/year as of Nov 2023; total bandwidth US$2.8m/year; temporary storage about US$1.3m/year; about 50 full-time staff costing about US$19m/year; total projected about US$50m/year by 2025 — [Signal blog](https://signal.org/blog/signal-is-expensive/); [CyberInsider summary](https://cyberinsider.com/signal-estimates-operational-costs-to-reach-50-million-by-2025/); [Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Signal-How-much-it-costs-to-run-a-messenger-app.770125.0.html)
- Signal's voice/video relaying reportedly uses about 20 petabytes/year costing about US$1.7m/year (figure reached me only via forum quotations of the blog, consistent with my recollection of the post but not re-read) — [Lemmy thread quoting the post](https://lemmy.nekusoul.de/post/142885); primary: [Signal blog](https://signal.org/blog/signal-is-expensive/)
- Mumble per-user bandwidth: 10-40 kbit/s each way per the Mumble FAQ as quoted on ServerFault; cap about 64 kbit/s per user; server RAM/disk needs "fairly minimal" — [ServerFault mirror](https://kiwix.lema.org/serverfault.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/226239/what-is-mumbles-average-monthly-bandwidth-usage); [ServerFault mirror 2](https://kiwix.lema.org/serverfault.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/11635/how-many-resources-does-a-mumble-server-need); [IONOS guide](https://www.ionos.com/digitalguide/server/know-how/mumble-server-setup/)
- Vendor estimate: one hour of relayed Opus voice is about 30 MB both directions; relays are needed for roughly 20% of WebRTC sessions (vendor blogs, medium reliability) — [ExpressTURN](https://blog.expressturn.com/stun-and-turn-the-basics-of-webrtc-connectivity); [Fora Soft TURN calculator, 2026](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/video-streaming/articles-streaming/turn-bandwidth-calculator)
- Tailscale: most traffic flows directly peer-to-peer; DERP relays are used for connection set-up and as fallback; relayed paths add latency and reduce throughput — [Tailscale DERP docs](https://tailscale.com/kb/1232/derp-servers); [Tailscale connection types](https://tailscale.com/docs/reference/connection-types)
- Tin Can: a 100x day-on-day spike took a venture-funded calling service down for about two weeks — [Tin Can update](https://tincan.kids/pages/service-update-jan-7-2026)
- Wyze, 16 Feb 2024: after an outage, a newly added third-party caching library overloaded on mass reconnect and mixed device/user IDs; about 13,000 users were shown other people's camera thumbnails and 1,504 tapped them — an example of a reconnect storm turning an outage into a privacy breach — [GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2024/wyze-security-cam-incident-that-exposed-images-to-other-users-impacts-more-than-13000-customers/); [Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/news/wyze-confirmed-a-camera-issue-last-week-caused-13000-owners-to-see-videos-from-other-homes/)

### Inferences
(All arithmetic is mine; assumptions: 46 kbit/s on the wire while talking, 20 min talk per family per day, relay receives one stream and forwards it to each listener.)
- Per family per day: 1,200 s x 46 kbit/s = 55 Mbit = about 6.9 MB into the relay, and the same out per listening device. Per month: about 207 MB in, about 207 MB out per listener.
- 10,000 families: about 2.1 TB/month egress with one listener each (about 6 TB with three listeners). Average load about 139 simultaneous talkers = about 6.4 Mbit/s; if the evening peak is 10x average, about 64 Mbit/s.
- 100,000 families: about 21 TB/month egress per listener (about 62 TB with three). Average about 64 Mbit/s; 10x peak about 640 Mbit/s and about 14,000 concurrent streams (about 700k packets/s inbound at 20 ms Opus frames; a third of that at 60 ms frames).
- Cost order of magnitude: at Signal's implied rate (about US$1.7m / 20 PB = about US$0.085/GB, close to hyperscaler list egress) 100,000 families is about 250 TB/year = about US$21k/year per listener fan-out; on flat-rate/included-traffic hosts it would be far less. 100,000 families is about 1.2% of Signal's reported call-relay volume. Server count: 10,000 families fits on one small VM plus a standby; 100,000 families is a handful of relay nodes (2-6) for redundancy and packet rate rather than CPU — this is an engineering estimate, not a published benchmark, and needs a load test.
- Idle-device cost (heartbeats, presence, NAT keepalives for 100k-300k always-on devices) is a connection-count problem, not a bandwidth one; UDP keepalives every 25-30 s at about 100 bytes are well under 1 Mbit/s in total even at 300k devices.
- Therefore bandwidth/compute is NOT the main commercial risk. The Signal split (US$19m people vs US$2.8m bandwidth) and the Tin Can/Wyze incidents point to the real costs: (a) someone on call when grandparents' radios go silent; (b) thundering-herd events (Christmas activation, post-outage mass reconnect, a bad firmware rollout); (c) the firmware-update channel, which is the one component that can brick the whole fleet and the highest-value target for an attacker; (d) patching the relay host and rotating keys; (e) DoS against a single well-known UDP endpoint; (f) customer support for Wi-Fi changes, which exists with or without a relay.
- Removing the relay does not remove (c) or (f), and the offline/low-battery alert feature inherently needs some always-on third party.

### Gaps
- No published capacity figures (connections or Mbit/s per core) found for Tailscale DERP, coturn, Jitsi Videobridge, Mumble or Zello/WhatsApp relays in this session; the sizing above is unbenchmarked.
- Signal's "20 PB / US$1.7m" call-bandwidth figure not confirmed from the primary page (signal.org unreachable).
- DDoS mitigation pricing for raw UDP (as opposed to HTTP) in/near NZ not researched; UDP protection is usually a paid tier and is a plausible hidden cost.

## 4. Security: blind relay versus P2P

### Takeaway
Both models have had serious incidents, but the worst consumer-IoT failures came from vendor P2P brokering platforms (ThroughTek Kalay) and from cloud services that could see content (Wyze); an end-to-end-encrypted blind relay limits a breach to metadata and availability. P2P does not eliminate central infrastructure (rendezvous/STUN is still needed) and adds IP-address exposure between peers.

### Cited Findings
- ThroughTek Kalay P2P SDK, CVE-2021-28372 (disclosed Aug 2021 by Mandiant/CISA, CVSS 9.6): an attacker who knew a device UID could register it on the Kalay network, hijack connections and obtain credentials for live audio/video; platform claimed 83 million active devices (number actually affected unknown); fixed in SDK 3.1.10+ — [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2021/08/17/cve-2021-28372/); [SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/millions-iot-devices-exposed-attacks-due-cloud-platform-vulnerability/); [GovInfoSecurity](https://www.govinfosecurity.com/fireeye-cisa-warn-critical-iot-device-vulnerability-a-17311)
- Wyze Feb 2024 cross-account exposure of about 13,000 users' camera thumbnails/videos through a server-side caching fault — possible only because the cloud handled plaintext media — [9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2024/02/19/wyze-camera-thumbnails-breach/); [CEPro](https://www.cepro.com/security/surveillance-cameras/wyze-outage-leads-to-security-incident-13000-user-camera-feeds-exposed/)
- Apple's FaceTime-based Walkie-Talkie is described as end-to-end encrypted with audio not stored on servers (secondary blog; treat with caution) — [Leapp guide](https://leapp.nl/en/blogs/iphone/apple-watch-walkietalkie-communiceer-snel-en-efficient-handige-gids)
- Syncthing's relay model: relays are blind transports that anyone can run; a relay that joins the default pool is publicly visible and usable by any client; a private relay can require a shared-secret token — [Syncthing relay server docs](https://docs.syncthing.net/users/strelaysrv)

### Inferences
- Kalay shows that "P2P" consumer products still depend on a central registration/rendezvous service, and that identity binding at that service (device UID as the only secret) is the critical weakness. The same class of bug applies to this project's relay if device identity is a guessable ID rather than a key pair: enumeration and impersonation are the blind relay's main risks, alongside metadata (who talks to whom, when, from which IP) and DoS.
- Blind-relay residual exposure: traffic metadata, the 24 h stored (encrypted) last message, alert contact details, and the firmware-signing path. A relay compromise cannot yield audio if keys never leave the devices, but a compromised update channel can — so firmware signing keys held offline matter more than relay hardening.
- P2P residual exposure: each family member's home IP address is disclosed to the others (low concern within a family); inbound hole-punching fails on a meaningful minority of networks (vendor figure about 20% need relays; NZ CGNAT on mobile/fixed-wireless and some fibre plans makes this worse), so a fallback relay is needed anyway; opening ports/UPnP on an elderly person's router enlarges that home's attack surface and is not something to ask of them.
- Net: for this threat model a hardened blind relay is the lower-risk design; P2P is a resilience/cost optimisation, not a security improvement.

### Gaps
- Eufy (Nov-Dec 2022 unencrypted cloud thumbnails/streams despite "local only" claims) and Ring (FTC 2023 order) not verified this session — UNVERIFIED background.
- No documented breach of an E2E-blind voice relay (e.g. a Signal TURN or Tailscale DERP compromise yielding content) was found; absence of evidence, not proof.
- Share of NZ residential connections behind CGNAT not researched.

## 5. "Survives its vendor" design patterns

### Takeaway
The patterns with real precedent are: a user-changeable server address plus a published self-hostable server (the OpenMoxie rescue, done in advance), community-run blind relay pools (Syncthing), and local operation that keeps working without the cloud (Insteon's direct links). For non-technical elderly users the credible versions are those where a family member or successor organisation flips one setting — not ones requiring the grandparent, or every family, to run infrastructure.

### Cited Findings
- OpenMoxie worked only for robots that had received the enabling OTA before shutdown, is unsupported by the vendor, and restores partial function — [AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/24/12/20/moxie-robot-may-be-saved-by-a-last-minute-open-sourcing-effort); [Fight to Repair](https://fighttorepair.substack.com/p/moxie-libre-push-to-open-source-may)
- Insteon devices with direct local links kept working after the cloud vanished; hub features and onboarding did not; users fell back to open-source controllers (Home Assistant/openHAB) — [TinkerTry](https://tinkertry.com/insteon-turns-off-cloud-but-local-controls-still-work); [TechCentral.ie](https://www.techcentral.ie/insteon-customers-forced-to-rely-on-open-source-fixes-after-apparent-collapse-of-iot-firm/); [openHAB forum](https://community.openhab.org/t/insteon-the-company-is-dead-can-the-hub-be-redirected/135126)
- Insteon's service was later revived by a customer group that acquired the assets — continuity via successor operator — [Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/INSTEON)
- Syncthing: community-contributed relays auto-join a public pool (relays.syncthing.net); operators may instead keep a relay private with a token — [Syncthing docs](https://docs.syncthing.net/users/strelaysrv)
- Tailscale now offers "peer relays" (a customer's own node acting as relay) as a faster alternative to vendor DERP — a mainstream example of customer-hosted fallback relays — [Tailscale connection types](https://tailscale.com/docs/reference/connection-types)

### Inferences
Ranking for families of non-technical elders (my assessment):
- Credible, low cost: (1) relay address and relay public key stored as device configuration changeable via the existing set-up/captive portal or QR card, not compiled in; (2) relay source published under the existing licence with a one-command deploy (cheap VPS / home Raspberry Pi), since the relay is blind and stateless apart from 24 h messages; (3) onboarding and pairing that never require the vendor (avoid Insteon's reset trap); (4) a written end-of-service commitment: N months' notice, final firmware that unlocks self-pointing, and signing-key/source escrow or hand-over to a successor/community operator; (5) relay list with failover (primary vendor relay, secondary community or family relay) so a successor can take over without touching devices.
- Credible as an enhancement: same-LAN direct mode (mDNS/ESP-NOW) — keeps radios in one house or rest-home working with no internet, but does not help the core grandparent-to-family-across-town case.
- Weak fit: full federation and pure internet P2P (NAT failure rate, no place for offline alerts or 24 h message hold); community relay pools at small scale (who runs them, and under what legal status — see section 6); Meshtastic-style shared public MQTT.
- One "family tech person" hosting a relay for several households is the realistic self-host unit; expecting each elderly user's household to host is not.
- These patterns also convert the CGA durability risk from "device dies with vendor" to "device needs a one-time re-point", which is a far stronger position at point of sale.

### Gaps
- Pebble/Rebble (community web services after Pebble's 2018 server shutdown) and Meshtastic public MQTT policies were not retrieved this session — UNVERIFIED background: Rebble kept Pebble watches working via a replacement boot/config URL and an optional paid subscription for costly services; Meshtastic's public MQTT server imposes restrictions (e.g. zero-hop, limited topics) because of abuse/load.
- No evidence found on how many ordinary households actually complete a self-host migration after a shutdown (OpenMoxie adoption numbers unknown).
- Software escrow practice for consumer IoT in NZ not researched.

## 6. New Zealand legal and regulatory position (NOT legal advice; significant uncertainty)

### Takeaway
On the text found, a vendor operating a voice relay for NZ end-users plausibly falls within TICSA at least as a "service provider" (duty to assist; no registration duty) and arguably — but uncertainly — as a "network operator" (registration, interception-capability and network-security duties); a hardware-only vendor whose customers host or go P2P looks to fall outside both. Privacy Act, CGA, radio (RSM) and electrical-safety duties attach to selling the device regardless of architecture. A short formal opinion and a direct question to the Police TICSA registrar are warranted before commercialising.

### Cited Findings
- TICSA 2013 s 3: "network operator means (a) a person who owns, controls, or operates a public telecommunications network; or (b) a person who supplies (whether by wholesale or retail) another person with the capability to provide a telecommunications service"; a public telecommunications network includes a "public data network", defined as a data network used, or intended for use, in whole or in part, by the public — [legislation.govt.nz, TICSA 2013](https://legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2013/91/en/latest/)
- "Service provider" is anyone who "provides or makes available in New Zealand a telecommunications service to an end-user" but "does not include a network operator" — [NZ Police TICSA page](https://www.police.govt.nz/advice-services/businesses-and-organisations/telecommunications-interception-capability-security-act)
- Network operators must register (register kept by NZ Police for the surveillance agencies); service providers are not obliged to register but are encouraged to do so voluntarily — [NZ Police TICSA page](https://www.police.govt.nz/advice/businesses-and-organisations/ticsa)
- TICSA has two limbs: interception capability (Part 2) and network security (Part 3, administered by GCSB/NCSC) — [NCSC About TICSA](https://www.ncsc.govt.nz/what-we-do/regulations-and-standards/ticsa-network-security/about-ticsa/); [GCSB](https://www.gcsb.govt.nz/news/telecommunications-interception-capability-and-security-act-2013-ticsa)
- Section 24 is headed "Duty to assist" (bill version applied it to "a network operator or a service provider" shown a warrant/authority); section 38 is headed "Minister may require service providers to have same obligations as network operators" — [TICSA 2013, current to 1 Oct 2025](https://legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2013/91/en/2025-10-01.pdf); [Bill as reported](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/bill/government/2013/108/en/latest)
- Over-the-top services were a live issue when the Act was passed: the TCF submitted that OTT services should be covered via obligations on the OTT provider or not at all, and TUANZ warned the Act could reach overseas app providers and encryption — [TCF submission 2013](https://www.tcf.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2013-06-TCF-TICSA-Submission-2013.pdf); [TUANZ](https://tuanz.org.nz/tag/interception/)
- Police publish class exemptions under TICSA, showing the regime has an exemption mechanism — [Police class exemption notice](https://www.police.govt.nz/about-us/publication/telecommunications-interception-capability-and-security-act-2013-class)
- 111: the Commerce Commission 111 Contact Code (Telecommunications Act 2001 s 238-239; updated July 2024) applies to providers of retail *landline* services — voice services over fixed-line or fixed-wireless — and concerns vulnerable consumers' ability to reach 111 in a power cut — [NZ Gazette notice](https://gazette.govt.nz/notice/id/2020-au1101); [TCF 111 Contact Code](https://www.tcf.org.nz/landline/111-contact-code)
- Radio: RSM uses supplier self-declaration — the NZ supplier (importer/manufacturer/seller) must hold evidence of conformity with prescribed standards, a Supplier's Declaration of Conformity, and label the product (RCM, or R-NZ for NZ-only radio transmitters in conformity levels A1-A3); test report or other reasonable evidence required, accredited report at the high-risk level — [RSM compliance guide](https://www.rsm.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/documents/compliance-guide.pdf); [RSM why compliance matters](https://www.rsm.govt.nz/business-individuals/supplier-compliance/why-compliance-matters); [RSM SDoC form](https://www.rsm.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/documents/Forms/RSM_-_Suppliers_declaration_of_Conformity.pdf)
- Module vendors do file NZ SDoCs for Wi-Fi modules (example: ST module, SDoC valid to 22 Jun 2030), and finished-product makers such as Raspberry Pi publish their own NZ declarations — [ST SDoC](https://www.st.com/resource/en/certification_document/rsmd250661-001.pdf); [Raspberry Pi NZ declaration](https://pip-assets.raspberrypi.com/categories/547-approvals/documents/RP-000035-CF-1-New%20Zealand.pdf)
- CGA durability/acceptable-quality guarantee applies to tech products (see section 2) — [Consumer Protection NZ](https://consumerprotection.govt.nz/general-help/consumer-laws/consumer-guarantees-act)

### Inferences
- Relay operator status (uncertain). A relay reachable only over the internet is more naturally a telecommunications *service* supplied to end-users than a "public telecommunications network"; on that reading the vendor is a "service provider": no registration and no standing interception-capability build, but a duty to assist a surveillance agency holding a warrant, and exposure to being "deemed in" by Ministerial direction under s 38. The contrary reading — that operating forwarding infrastructure "intended for use by the public" is operating a public data network, making the vendor a network operator with registration and Part 2/3 duties — cannot be excluded on the definitions alone. The Act's small-operator relief (see Gaps) would matter if so.
- End-to-end encryption. If the vendor is only ever able to hand over ciphertext and metadata, "assistance" is in practice limited to metadata, connection records and the stored encrypted last message. The design choice that device keys are generated on-device and never held by the vendor is what keeps this true; any key-escrow or vendor-side pairing shortcut would change it. Note the firmware-update channel is a theoretical lever for compelled access in any jurisdiction; whether TICSA could compel a modified build was not researched.
- P2P or customer-hosted. If the vendor only sells hardware and firmware, and any relay is run by the family (or audio flows directly), the vendor provides no telecommunications service to end-users and so appears to be outside both definitions; a family running a private relay for itself is not serving "the public". A vendor-run rendezvous/STUN or alert service still is a service, though a thinner one. A *community* relay pool open to all buyers would put the volunteer operators, not the vendor, in the ambiguous position.
- 111. The device assigns no phone numbers, does not interconnect with the PSTN and is not a landline replacement, so the 111 Contact Code does not appear to apply. The practical legal risk is instead consumer-law (Fair Trading Act/CGA fitness for purpose) if marketing implies it is a safety or emergency device; the existing explicit "not an emergency alarm" position should be carried onto packaging and the offline/low-battery alerts described as convenience features without reliability promises.
- Privacy Act 2020 applies to any relay operator holding personal information (account/contact details for alerts, device identifiers, IP addresses, talk-time metadata, the encrypted stored message). Minimising retention (e.g. no connection logs beyond operational need) directly shrinks this exposure; a no-relay design shrinks it to sales/customer records.
- Radio/electrical duties are identical for relay and no-relay designs. A pre-certified ESP32-S3 module supplies most of the radio evidence, but the NZ supplier of the finished product still needs its own SDoC/compliance folder and label, and EMC of the complete product (and electrical safety of any mains charger supplied) remains the supplier's responsibility. 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi operation relies on the short-range-device general user licence rather than an individual licence.
- Overall: the relay adds (i) probable TICSA service-provider status, (ii) a larger Privacy Act footprint, and (iii) CGA durability exposure tied to service continuity. None looks prohibitive for a small NZ operator, and (iii) is mitigated by the section 5 patterns. The relay is a real but not dominant legal risk; product-safety, CGA and support obligations exist either way.

### Gaps
- TICSA wording not read in full. UNVERIFIED background knowledge to be checked against the statute: s 10 limits the decryption duty to encryption the operator itself provided and does not require an operator to decrypt encryption supplied by others or to hold keys; operators with fewer than 4,000 customers have reduced ("intercept ready/accessible") obligations; Part 3 network-security notification duties apply to network operators; penalties are pecuniary via the High Court. No Police/NCSC statement was found on whether E2E OTT app providers are treated as network operators, and no evidence that any OTT messaging provider is on the register.
- Privacy Act 2020 not retrieved. UNVERIFIED background: 13 information privacy principles (collection limitation, security safeguards IPP 5, retention IPP 9, overseas disclosure IPP 12 with a carve-out where an overseas cloud host merely holds data as agent), mandatory notification of privacy breaches likely to cause serious harm, privacy officer requirement, and a new IPP 3A on indirect collection commencing 1 May 2026. Source to check: [Privacy Act 2020](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/0031/latest/LMS23223.html) and privacy.org.nz.
- Whether the Telecommunications Act 2001 imposes anything else on an app-style voice service (e.g. Telecommunications Development Levy liability threshold, Commission retail service quality codes, TDR scheme membership) was not researched; UNVERIFIED recollection is that the levy applies only above a NZ$10m telecommunications revenue threshold.
- RSM: the applicable standard reference for 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi/BLE, the conformity level, whether a module's SDoC can be relied on for a host product without retest, and the General User Radio Licence for Short Range Devices were not confirmed. Electrical safety (Electricity (Safety) Regulations 2010, declared medium-risk articles such as external power supplies) not researched; shipping lithium cells adds transport rules, also not researched.
- No NZ case or regulator guidance found on CGA liability for a discontinued cloud service; Fair Trading Act not researched.
- Whether a "family project gifted to relatives" (current status) is "in trade"/"supply" for CGA, RSM or TICSA purposes was not researched; obligations discussed assume commercial sale.
