# Direct internet P2P voice between ESP32-S3 units on home Wi-Fi (NAT traversal, residual infrastructure, NZ reliability)

Research date: 14 November 2026.

**Verification caveat for the report writer.** Page fetches were blocked in this session (2degrees.nz, apnic.net, arxiv.org, tailscale.com and components.espressif.com all failed to resolve), so every cited finding below rests on web-search result extracts of the linked page, not a full read of it. ## 1. NAT reality for NZ home broadband (CGNAT, static IPv4, IPv6) and the share of home pairs that could connect directly

### Takeaway
CGNAT is no longer a mobile-only problem in NZ: 2degrees puts all dynamic-IP home broadband behind CGNAT, fixed-wireless (about 20% of connections) and Starlink (about 3%, 19% of rural) are CGNAT by default, and IPv6 is patchy (2degrees about 46%, One NZ about 39%, Spark about 0.6% of users). CGNAT does not by itself stop hole punching, so a rough, unverified estimate is that 75 to 95% of NZ home-to-home pairs could connect directly, leaving a tail that cannot.

### Cited Findings
- 2degrees: "If you've got a broadband connection with a Dynamic IP broadband connection (not a Static IP), then you'll be moved to CGNAT. Customers with a Static IP are not affected." A home static IP costs NZ$10 a month, by email or phone request; business customers get one at no extra charge. The page lists remote access, DDNS and VPN servers as things CGNAT breaks. — [2degrees IP Addresses](https://2degrees.nz/help/broadband-help/ip-addresses/2degrees-ip-addresses)
- Spark wireless broadband is behind CGNAT by default; a static IP can be added by request form. A forum user put the price at NZ$10 a month (old figure). With a Spark static IP, TCP 25 and TCP/UDP 53 stay blocked by default. — [Geekzone: Spark Rural wireless Broadband and CGNAT](https://www.geekzone.co.nz/forums.asp?forumid=39&topicid=238128); [Geekzone: Wireless broadband, static IP, open ports](https://www.geekzone.co.nz/forums.asp?forumid=39&topicid=243209)
- Spark's service-restrictions help page recommends a Static IP add-on where CGNAT affects the customer. The extract did not say which products are CGNAT. — [Spark service restrictions](https://www.spark.co.nz/help/internet/learn/service-restrictions/)

- Technology mix (Commerce Commission data via the TCF 2025 annual report): fibre 74% of connections, fixed wireless 20%, LEO satellite 3% of the market and 19% of rural connections; 92,000 copper lines left at 30 June 2025. ### Inferences
- Homes behind IPv4 CGNAT, roughly: fixed wireless 20% + satellite 3% + 2degrees dynamic-IP fibre (about 22% share of a 74% fibre base, so up to about 16%) gives something like 35 to 40% of NZ homes. This multiplies figures from different sources and bases; treat as order of magnitude only.
- CGNAT is not the same as "cannot hole punch". What matters is whether the outermost NAT keeps one external port per internal socket (endpoint-independent mapping, "easy") or picks a new port per destination (symmetric, "hard"). Fixed-line CGNAT is usually built to the easy behaviour; mobile-core CGNAT (which NZ 4G/5G fixed wireless rides on) is the likelier hard case. Unsourced general knowledge, and no NZ measurement was found.
- Rough pair estimate: if 10 to 15% of homes sit behind a hard NAT (mostly fixed wireless, perhaps Starlink), a pair fails only when both are hard in the best case (about 1 to 2% of pairs) or when either is hard in the worst case (about 20 to 28% of pairs). That brackets 75 to 95%+ direct, which sits between the libp2p (70%) and Tailscale (over 90%) figures in section 2. This is my estimate, not a measured number.
- IPv6 will rescue few pairs today: both homes need working IPv6, and with Spark (largest ISP) near zero, the chance both ends have it is perhaps 10 to 15% of pairs. It helps most exactly where it is needed if the CGNAT ISP (2degrees, Starlink) also gives IPv6.
- Two units in the same 2degrees or fixed-wireless CGNAT pool may need the CGNAT to hairpin (route internal-to-internal via the public address). Hairpin support varies and was not verifiable for any NZ ISP.
- A product cannot ask a grandparent to buy a NZ$10 a month static IP, so static IP is a rescue for known-bad homes, not a design basis.

### Gaps
- One NZ fibre/HFC CGNAT policy and static IP price: no help page or recent thread surfaced. Spark fibre CGNAT status in 2026 also unconfirmed (older community belief is public dynamic IPv4).
- Contact, Mercury, Orcon/Slingshot (2degrees group), Voyager, rural WISPs: no current sources retrieved. Voyager and some smaller ISPs are widely believed to give public IPv4 and IPv6 by default (unsourced).
- Google's per-country IPv6 figure for NZ was not retrieved; APNIC dates were not visible.
- No measurement of NAT mapping behaviour (easy vs hard) on any NZ CGNAT, nor of Starlink's CGNAT type. This is the single most valuable thing to test in the field: a STUN two-server check from each candidate home.

## 2. UDP hole-punching success rates in practice, failure modes, and whether IPv6 fixes it

### Takeaway
Measured direct-connection rates run from about 70% (libp2p DCUtR, open internet, 4.4 million attempts) to "well north of 90%" (Tailscale's internal figure, with more tricks), and WebRTC services typically relay 10 to 25% of calls. A relay fallback is needed for the tail unless the product accepts that some pairs never connect; IPv6 removes address translation but not the stateful firewall, so both ends must still send first.

### Cited Findings
- libp2p DCUtR measurement (Trautwein, Ihle, Schubotz, Gipp): over 4.4 million traversal attempts from more than 85,000 networks; hole-punch stage success 70% ± 7.1%, conditional on relay reservation and public address discovery having succeeded; TCP and QUIC statistically indistinguishable at about 70%; 97.6% of successes came on the first attempt; outcome independent of the relay's RTT or location. Preprint October 2025, accepted to IMC '26. — [arXiv 2510.27500](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27500v1); [arXiv 2604.12484](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12484); [ProbeLab publication page](https://probelab.io/publications/large-scale-measurement-of-nat-traversal-for-the-decentralized-web-a-case-study-of-dcutr-in-ipfs/)
- A third-party summary of the same paper says about 29% of attempts failed before reaching the hole-punch stage. — [Moonlight review](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/large-scale-measurement-of-nat-traversal-for-the-decentralized-web-a-case-study-of-dcutr-in-ipfs)
- ProbeLab follow-up: port-restricted cone NATs reached 82.9% success among DCUtR-eligible peers (different cohort from the 70% aggregate). — [ProbeLab: Can libp2p punch through NATs?](https://probelab.io/blog/can-libp2p-punch-through-nats/)
- Tailscale (15 October 2025): internal metrics show direct NAT traversal success "well north of 90%" in typical conditions; connections start on the DERP relay and upgrade. Vendor figure, not independently measured. — [Tailscale: NAT traversal improvements pt 1](https://tailscale.com/blog/nat-traversal-improvements-pt-1)
- Tailscale (26 January 2026) concedes that with CGNAT combined with double NAT from home routers, "Tailscale's NAT traversal simply can't establish direct connections in many cases"; Peer Relays (beta 29 October 2025) let a customer's own nodes relay over UDP because the TCP-based DERP path adds latency and limits throughput. — [Tailscale: Peer relays and international networks](https://tailscale.com/blog/peer-relays-international-networks); [Tailscale: Peer relays beta](https://tailscale.com/blog/peer-relays-beta)
- Tailscale's connectivity matrix: easy NAT to easy NAT connects directly; pairs with a hard NAT on one or both sides are relayed (unless port mapping or other help applies). — [Tailscale docs: Device connectivity](https://tailscale.com/docs/reference/device-connectivity)
- Mechanics (Tailscale article reposted by APNIC): easy NATs reuse one public port for all destinations so a STUN-learned port works for any peer; hard NATs allocate per destination. Easy-to-hard can be attacked with birthday-paradox probing (256 ports opened on the hard side; roughly 50% success in about 2 s and over 99% in about 20 s at 100 probes a second, per secondary write-ups); hard-to-hard needs on the order of 170,000 probes and is impractical. — [APNIC blog: How NAT traversal works](https://blog.apnic.net/2022/04/26/how-nat-traversal-works-nat-notes-for-nerds/); [Pinggy summary](https://pinggy.io/blog/how_nat_traversal_works/)
- WebRTC relay share: Philipp Hancke's 2016 dataset showed 12.1% of connected calls on TURN/UDP, 5% on TURN/TCP, under 0.5% on TURN/TLS; industry estimates since cluster at 10 to 25% ("most large vendors report 20% TURN relay traffic"). No reliable 2024 to 2026 public figure was found. — [Hancke: What kind of TURN server is being used?](https://medium.com/@fippo/what-kind-of-turn-server-is-being-used-d67dbfc2ff5d); [discuss-webrtc thread](https://groups.google.com/g/discuss-webrtc/c/5d_EJwM6iJM); [Forasoft glossary](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/video-streaming/glossary/terms-streaming/turn)
- iroh: docs say hole punching works "in most configurations" with relay fallback for some corporate firewalls and cellular networks; a GitHub issue reports success depends heavily on UPnP or public IPv6 being available; third-party posts quote both about 90% and about 70%. — [iroh docs: holepunching](https://docs.iroh.computer/concepts/holepunching); [iroh issue 2317](https://github.com/n0-computer/iroh/issues/2317); [Pinggy on iroh 1.0](https://pinggy.io/blog/iroh_1_0_dial_keys_not_ips/)
- IPv6: RFC 6092 (January 2011, informational) recommends "simple security" in residential IPv6 routers; in practice most routers block unsolicited inbound IPv6 by default and allow established/related return traffic (OpenWrt WAN default: input REJECT, forward REJECT). — [RFC 6092](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6092.html); [OneUptime: IPv6 firewall on home routers (2026)](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-03-20-ipv6-firewall-home-routers/view)
- Port mapping: a 2019 Trend Micro scan found UPnP enabled on 76% of home routers. Old, and it measures enablement, not mapping success. — [Trend Micro: UPnP-enabled home devices](https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/research/19/c/upnp-enabled-connected-devices-in-home-unpatched-known-vulnerabilities.html)

### Inferences
- The libp2p 70% is the pessimistic bound for this product: it covers a global mix of arbitrary networks. The Tailscale 90%+ is the optimistic bound: it adds port mapping, birthday probing, IPv6 and constant re-tries, and still relies on a relay for the rest. A home-Wi-Fi-only fleet in NZ should land between the two (see section 1).
- What fails: (a) hard/symmetric NAT on both sides; (b) hard plus port-restricted easy NAT without birthday probing; (c) double NAT where the inner router is fine but the outer CGNAT is hard; (d) two units behind the same CGNAT with no hairpin; (e) networks that block UDP outright (rare at home, common in rest homes, hospitals and corporate guest Wi-Fi, which matters for a grandparent product); (f) short UDP mapping timeouts that need keepalives every 15 to 30 s.
- IPv6 hole punching is easier than IPv4 (no port prediction; the address the unit sees is the address the peer dials), but it is still "both send at the same time after a rendezvous", so signalling is still needed. Routers that follow RFC 6092 statefully should pass it; no measured IPv6 hole-punch success rate was found.
- Port mapping (UPnP-IGD, NAT-PMP, PCP) on the home router turns a port-restricted home into an open one, but it does nothing when the home router's WAN address is itself a CGNAT address (100.64.0.0/10), which is exactly the hard case in NZ.
- PTT has an advantage over calls: traffic is bursty and tolerant of a 1 to 2 s setup if a path is kept warm with keepalives, so hole punching can be done once at boot and maintained, not per talk burst.

### Gaps
- No public 2024 to 2026 WebRTC statistic on TURN share from Google, Twilio or callstats was retrieved.
- No study of hole punching specifically between residential fixed-line networks, and none for NZ.
- RFC 4787 (NAT UDP behaviour) and RFC 8445 (ICE) are the relevant standards but were not retrieved in this session (unsourced mention).

## 3. What runs on ESP32-S3 today

### Takeaway
A full ICE/STUN/TURN stack with Opus audio is available and maintained for ESP32-S3 (Espressif esp_peer in esp-webrtc-solution, and the MIT-licensed libpeer it derives from), and iroh now officially lists ESP32-S3 with PSRAM as able to do relay plus address lookup. libp2p, Tox and OpenDHT have no credible ESP32 ports; UPnP exists only as hobby-grade libraries and no NAT-PMP/PCP client was found.

### Cited Findings
- Espressif esp_peer (latest seen 1.5.8, frequent releases through 2026): WebRTC peer stack with TURN (RFC 5766 and RFC 8656), controlling or controlled ICE role, data channel, RTP jitter buffers; "even on platforms without PSRAM, a minimal setup uses < 60 KB RAM"; the `peer_demo` example runs two peers at once on an ESP32-S3 without external memory; tunable through `esp_peer_default_cfg_t`. — [esp_peer 1.5.8 README](https://components.espressif.com/components/espressif/esp_peer/versions/1.5.8/readme); [esp_peer 1.5.6 README](https://components.espressif.com/components/espressif/esp_peer/versions/1.5.6/readme)
- sepfy libpeer: MIT licence; Opus, G.711, H.264, DataChannel, STUN/TURN, IPv4/IPv6, WHIP and MQTT signalling; depends on mbedtls, libsrtp, usrsctp, cJSON, coreHTTP, coreMQTT; ESP32 example needs ESP-IDF 5.2+ and targets an ESP32-S3 board. esp_peer is described as derived from libpeer with ESP32 optimisations. — [sepfy/libpeer](https://github.com/sepfy/libpeer); [libpeer on ESP Component Registry](https://components.espressif.com/components/sepfy/libpeer); [MIT licence text](https://components.espressif.com/components/sepfy/libpeer/versions/0.0.3/license)
- iroh on ESP32: compatibility page lists ESP32 (Xtensa and RISC-V) as supported. Boards without PSRAM (about 512 KiB SRAM) are LAN-direct only (ticket with IP, or mDNS by endpoint ID) because the relay client and pkarr address lookup do not fit; PSRAM boards including ESP32-S3 get relay plus address lookup. Devices under 8 MiB flash need the reduced-dependency build. Xtensa needs the `esp` Rust toolchain. A 2025 blog noted the stock build failed on ESP-IDF over missing features such as cmsg; the no-PSRAM variant is called a proof of concept. — [iroh compatibility](https://docs.iroh.computer/compatibility); [Running iroh on an ESP32](https://www.iroh.computer/blog/iroh-on-esp32); [iroh-esp32-examples](https://github.com/n0-computer/iroh-esp32-examples); [iroh-workshop-esp32](https://github.com/n0-computer/iroh-workshop-esp32)
- libp2p: an ESP32 remote-control write-up says go-libp2p-based tools are "beyond the reach of the chip" and runs them on a Linux intermediary. Informal source. — [aabbtree77/esp32-vpn](https://github.com/aabbtree77/esp32-vpn)
- WireGuard: trombik/esp_wireguard v0.9.0 for ESP-IDF (based on the lwIP WireGuard implementation, self-described alpha, single tunnel working, lists ESP-IDF v4.2 to 4.3); its maintained successor is esphome/wireguard (v0.4.6 on the registry); Tinkerforge WireGuard-ESP32-Arduino is BSD 3-clause. — [trombik/esp_wireguard](https://components.espressif.com/components/trombik/esp_wireguard); [esphome/wireguard](https://components.espressif.com/components/esphome/wireguard/versions/0.4.6/readme); [Tinkerforge WireGuard-ESP32-Arduino](https://github.com/Tinkerforge/WireGuard-ESP32-Arduino)
- UPnP-IGD: TinyUPnP (Arduino, ESP8266/ESP32, LGPL-2.1, last pushed 2024) and its derivative UPnP_Generic; one native ESP-IDF example (WIZnet wsm_driver) does SSDP M-SEARCH for InternetGatewayDevice:1 and WANIPConnection:1 add/delete mappings. No ESP-IDF NAT-PMP or PCP client surfaced. — [ofekp/TinyUPnP](https://github.com/ofekp/TinyUPnP); [khoih-prog/UPnP_Generic](https://www.github.com/khoih-prog/UPnP_Generic); [wsm_driver UPnP example](https://components.espressif.com/components/wiznet/wsm_driver/versions/1.1.1/examples/upnp)
- The project's planned base, xiaozhi-esp32 (MIT), already does Opus over MQTT plus encrypted UDP on ESP32 boards. — [78/xiaozhi-esp32](https://github.com/78/xiaozhi-esp32)

### Inferences
- Lowest-risk path to P2P media: keep the existing sealed-Opus-over-UDP packet format and add only a small ICE-lite layer (STUN binding request, candidate exchange, connectivity checks, keepalives). STUN binding is a few hundred lines of C over lwIP UDP; a full WebRTC stack (DTLS-SRTP, SCTP) is not needed because the project already has ChaCha20-Poly1305 with a group key. esp_peer/libpeer are useful as reference code or if browser/phone interop is wanted.
- WireGuard on ESP32 gives encryption and roaming but no NAT traversal of its own: it needs one end reachable or an external coordinator, so it does not remove infrastructure. It is the mechanism Tailscale wraps, not a substitute for it.
- iroh is the closest thing to "P2P in a box" on ESP32-S3 (dial by public key, hole punching, relay fallback, pkarr/DNS discovery), but it is Rust on Xtensa, QUIC-based, young on this platform, and still leans on relays run by n0 or by the product owner. Mixing it with the existing C core adds toolchain cost.
- NAT-PMP and PCP are simple UDP request/response protocols and could be hand-written in a day; UPnP-IGD needs SSDP plus SOAP over HTTP, more code and more router quirks.
- mDNS for same-LAN discovery is a standard ESP-IDF component (unsourced; well known), so two units in one house need no internet at all.

### Gaps
- esp_peer licence terms were not confirmed (esp-webrtc-solution may ship parts as prebuilt libraries under Espressif's licence; check before commercial use). IPv6 candidate support in esp_peer not confirmed.
- Amazon KVS WebRTC C SDK on ESP32, Tox (toxcore), OpenDHT and Hyperswarm embedded ports: nothing retrieved; I am not aware of production-grade ESP32 ports of the last three (unsourced).
- No measured RAM/flash figure for libpeer or iroh on ESP32-S3 beyond "needs PSRAM" for iroh and "under 60 KB minimal" for esp_peer.

## 4. Irreducible infrastructure with P2P: STUN, signalling/rendezvous, TURN fallback

### Takeaway
P2P removes the media relay from the common path but not the need for three small services: address discovery (STUN), rendezvous (how peers learn each other's current address) and a relay of last resort. STUN and rendezvous can be someone else's free service or a near-zero-cost stateless one; a relay for the unpunchable tail cannot be had free at commercial scale without either running one or letting well-connected units relay for others.

### Cited Findings
- Public STUN: Google runs stun.l.google.com and stun1 to stun4; guides warn "these free STUN servers might not be available tomorrow, there is no SLA and no support" and vendors advise against relying on them as the primary mechanism in a commercial service. — [dev.to: Google STUN server list](https://dev.to/alakkadshaw/google-stun-server-list-21n4); [Metered: STUN servers and friends](https://www.metered.ca/tools/openrelay/stun-servers-and-friends/)
- Free TURN: Metered's Open Relay offers free TURN with sign-up; quota is quoted inconsistently as 20 GB or 50 GB a month; Metered's paid product includes unlimited STUN. Vendor claims. Self-hosting uses coturn. — [Open Relay](https://metered.ca/tools/openrelay/); [dev.to: free STUN and TURN list](https://dev.to/aprogrammer22/list-of-free-stun-and-turn-servers-open-relay-project-3a70); [Metered STUN/TURN](https://www.metered.ca/stun-turn)
- Cloudflare operates a managed TURN service (Realtime/Calls TURN); free-tier terms were not retrieved. — [Cloudflare TURN FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/turn/faq/index.md)
- libp2p's design shows the dependency chain: hole punching only starts after a relay reservation and public address discovery succeed, and about 29% of attempts fail before that stage. Decentralised does not mean infrastructure-free; it means the infrastructure is other peers. — [arXiv 2604.12484](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12484); [Moonlight review](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/large-scale-measurement-of-nat-traversal-for-the-decentralized-web-a-case-study-of-dcutr-in-ipfs)
- Tailscale always starts on a relay (DERP) and upgrades; Peer Relays move the fallback onto the customer's own well-placed nodes, with throughput near direct. — [Tailscale: NAT traversal improvements pt 1](https://tailscale.com/blog/nat-traversal-improvements-pt-1); [Tailscale: Peer relays beta](https://tailscale.com/blog/peer-relays-beta)
- iroh uses relays plus pkarr (signed DNS-style records, publishable to DNS or the BitTorrent mainline DHT) for address lookup, and mDNS on the LAN; iroh documents a dedicated-infrastructure option for running your own. — [iroh compatibility](https://docs.iroh.computer/compatibility); [iroh docs: NAT traversal](https://docs.iroh.computer/concepts/nat-traversal.md)
- libpeer ships MQTT and WHIP signalling, showing that a public or cheap MQTT broker is an accepted rendezvous channel for embedded WebRTC. — [sepfy/libpeer](https://github.com/sepfy/libpeer)
- The current design already notes the relay can be hosted free (Oracle Always Free) or for about NZ$4.50 to 9 a month on Vultr. — [Oracle Free Tier](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/Content/FreeTier/resourceref.htm)

### Inferences
- STUN: can be public (Google, Cloudflare, Metered and others), with a list of several operators baked into firmware and updatable by OTA. Per-unit load is one small UDP exchange every few tens of seconds. It can also be eliminated when units have IPv6 or a UPnP/PCP mapping, or by letting an already-connected group member report "this is the address I see you at" (peer-assisted STUN).
- Rendezvous options, least to most dependent on the product owner:
  1. mDNS on the same LAN: no infrastructure.
  2. Pairing-time exchange only (QR/USB): works only while addresses stay fixed, i.e. static IP or stable IPv6 prefix at one end. Not general.
  3. Dynamic DNS or signed records in someone else's DNS/DHT (pkarr on mainline DHT): no owner-run server, but ESP32 DHT clients are not available off the shelf outside iroh, and DHT bootstrap nodes are themselves third-party infrastructure.
  4. Public MQTT broker (HiveMQ, EMQX, Mosquitto test brokers): free, but no SLA, rate limits, and metadata (which units talk, when, from which IP) is visible to the broker unless the payload is sealed with the group key. Payload sealing is easy since the group key already exists.
  5. Owner-run rendezvous: a stateless mailbox keyed by group ID carrying sealed candidate blobs. This is tiny compared with a media relay (bytes per minute per unit, no audio, no keys) and could sit on a free tier or serverless function. It is still a central service, but one whose compromise reveals only IP addresses and timing.
- Relay fallback is the part that cannot be made to vanish. Choices: (a) accept that some pairs do not work and screen at set-up with a NAT test; (b) public free TURN (quota and terms unsuitable for a commercial product); (c) owner-run TURN/relay, carrying only the 5 to 25% tail, so capacity and cost fall by roughly 4 to 20 times against relaying everything; (d) peer relay: any group member with an easy NAT or IPv6 forwards sealed packets for the two that cannot reach each other. Option (d) needs no owner infrastructure and fits groups of 3+, but does nothing for a two-unit pair that is hard-to-hard.
- The 24-hour last-message store, presence/offline alerts and OTA are not NAT problems but they are the other reasons the current design has a server. Store-and-forward can move to the units (each keeps the last spurt and re-offers it when a peer reappears, which only works if the sender or another member is online when the sleeper wakes). Offline alerts to a caregiver's phone and OTA hosting need some always-on endpoint; OTA can be a static file on a CDN or GitHub release, signed by the owner.
- Net position: "no centrally maintained relay" is achievable for most pairs; "no central anything" is not, unless third-party public services with no SLA are accepted. The honest framing is a shift from a stateful, always-in-path media relay to a thin, mostly stateless rendezvous plus optional fallback.

### Gaps
- Who operates each public STUN server and their uptime history: no reliable source retrieved.
- Terms of service of public MQTT brokers for commercial device fleets were not checked.
- Cloudflare TURN free allowance and pricing not retrieved.

## 5. Group calls of 2 to 10 without a server, and distributed floor control

### Takeaway
Full mesh is feasible at this size (a talker sends at most 9 copies of a roughly 46 kbit/s stream, about 0.4 Mbit/s up), and 3GPP's off-network MCPTT floor control in TS 24.380 is a ready-made, arbiter-free model: request, wait briefly for objection, then announce "floor taken" and become the temporary arbitrator.

### Cited Findings
- TS 24.380 off-network flow (Annex A.4): a participant wanting the floor sends Floor Request and starts timer T201; the request is repeated a configured number of times; if no arbitrator responds, after the last T201 expiry the client sends Floor Taken to the others, assumes the floor arbitrator role and enters "has permission". — [TS 24.380 A.4 off-network signalling flows (itecspec mirror)](https://itecspec.com/spec/3gpp-24-380-a-4-off-network-floor-control-signalling-flows)
- If a Floor Queue Position Info arrives from the current arbitrator, the requester stops T201 and enters "queued"; received RTP media is rendered in that state. Pre-emptive Floor Request while the floor is taken is covered in A.4.2.4. — [TS 24.380 A.4](https://itecspec.com/spec/3gpp-24-380-a-4-off-network-floor-control-signalling-flows)
- T201 default is 40 ms, "depending on the D2D sidelink characteristics", set from initial UE configuration. — [TS 24.380 clause 11 configurable parameters](https://itecspec.com/spec/3gpp-24-380-11-configurable-parameters)
- The mirror is not the official 3GPP text and release may differ. — [3GPP TS 24.380 draft V17.1.0](https://3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_CT/WG1_mm-cc-sm_ex-CN1/TSGC1_127e/Inbox/Drafts/draft_24380-h10%2BJA.doc)
- Existing design targets: floor grant within 300 ms and mouth-to-ear under 300 ms (3GPP TS 22.179); wire rate about 46 kbit/s for 24 kbit/s Opus at 20 ms. — [TS 22.179 6.15.3.2](https://www.itecspec.com/3gpp/22.179/s/6.15.3.2)

### Inferences
- Mesh arithmetic: 10 units means 45 pairwise paths to punch and keep alive. If each pair independently succeeds with probability 0.85, the chance all 45 work is under 0.1%, so a mesh must tolerate missing edges by forwarding through a member both can reach (peer relay). With only one talker at a time, forwarding load is one stream.
- Host model alternative: one unit (the best connected, ideally with public IPv4/IPv6 or a UPnP mapping) acts as hub and floor arbiter, the others connect only to it. This cuts paths from 45 to 9 and makes floor control trivial, but the group dies when the hub is off, so it needs host election and hand-over. A hybrid (mesh where possible, any member forwards where not, floor by 24.380-style announcement) avoids a single point of failure.
- T201 at 40 ms assumes a radio sidelink. Over the internet between NZ homes, one-way delay of 10 to 40 ms plus jitter suggests a wait of perhaps 100 to 200 ms with two or three repeats, still inside the 300 ms grant target, especially since the design already records from button-down (pre-roll).
- Collisions (two presses inside the wait window) need a deterministic tie-break that every unit computes the same way: for example lowest (timestamp, unit ID), or caregiver priority first. The loser hears the busy tone and discards its pre-roll, as in the current design. The floor holder's own voice packets act as the implicit "floor taken" refresh; silence for 1.5 s or 60 s total releases it, as now.
- Partition risk: if A cannot reach C but both reach B, A and C can both believe the floor is free. Forwarding floor messages through B (gossip) fixes this for connected graphs.
- Open-mode (voice-activated) floor works the same way with voice activity standing in for the button.

### Gaps
- MANET push-to-talk literature and non-3GPP distributed floor control papers were not searched in the time available.
- The exact 24.380 procedure text for simultaneous requests (collision resolution by SSRC/priority) was not retrieved; check clause 7 of the official spec.
- No real-world measurement of ESP32-S3 handling 9 simultaneous sealed streams outbound (CPU for 9 ChaCha20-Poly1305 seals per 20 ms is small, but untested).

## 6. Security implications of P2P

### Takeaway
P2P trades a central target for exposure at the edges: every group member learns every other member's home IP address, units must accept unsolicited-looking UDP, and membership/re-keying loses its convenient trusted coordinator. None of this is disqualifying for family groups, but it changes the threat model and removes the remote re-key and revoke path the current relay provides.

### Cited Findings
- UPnP on home routers is a known attack surface: Trend Micro's 2019 scan found UPnP enabled on 76% of routers, 16% of those using the MiniUPnPd library, many with unpatched known vulnerabilities. — [Trend Micro: UPnP-enabled home devices](https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/research/19/c/upnp-enabled-connected-devices-in-home-unpatched-known-vulnerabilities.html)
- Residential IPv6 guidance (RFC 6092) exists precisely because, without NAT, hosts are directly addressable and rely on the router's stateful filter. — [RFC 6092](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6092.html)
- CGNAT ISPs and Starlink block inbound by policy, which protects units there but is also why they are hard to reach. — [Starlink support article](https://starlink.com/support/article/1192f3ef-2a17-31d9-261a-a59d215629f4); [2degrees IP Addresses](https://2degrees.nz/help/broadband-help/ip-addresses/2degrees-ip-addresses)
- In the current design the relay forwards sealed packets it cannot read, holds no group key, and is the place where re-keying, adding and dropping units happens without touching devices. — project docs: `/home/user/walkie-talkie/Rabbit R1 Walkie-Talkie Research and Design.md` and `/home/user/walkie-talkie/Walkie-Talkie Beyond the R1 Minimum Hardware.md`; packet sealing per [libsodium](https://doc.libsodium.org/)
- Tailscale frames NAT traversal as increasingly an access-control question (which node may relay for which), not only networking. Secondary summary. — [NHI Mgmt Group summary of Tailscale post](https://nhimg.org/articles/nat-traversal-is-becoming-an-access-control-problem-not-just-networking/)

### Inferences
- IP exposure: with a relay, members see only the relay's address. With P2P, each unit's public IP (roughly suburb-level location, ISP) is visible to all group members and to whatever rendezvous service is used. Fine inside a family; a concern for larger or semi-public groups in a commercial product. Peer-relayed members remain hidden from the far side.
- Open ports: plain hole punching opens no persistent port; the mapping exists only for peers the unit has itself contacted. UPnP/PCP mappings are persistent inbound holes to the unit and should be requested with short lifetimes, a random port, and removed on sleep. Many security-minded users disable UPnP, so it can only ever be an optimisation.
- DoS and abuse: a unit that listens for peers must drop anything that fails authentication cheaply. The existing per-packet ChaCha20-Poly1305 tag with the group key does this (one MAC check per packet); add a cheap pre-filter on group ID and a rate limit so a flood cannot starve the audio task. An ESP32 on home Wi-Fi cannot survive a volumetric flood, but neither can the home router; the relay design hid home IPs from outsiders, which P2P only partly does (IPs are known to members and the rendezvous service, not published).
- Rendezvous metadata: candidate blobs should be sealed with the group key so a public broker or DHT learns only that some group ID is active from some IP. A group ID derived from the key by hashing avoids a stable public identifier.
- Key distribution without a server: pairing by QR/USB/BLE at set-up still works and is the root of trust. What is lost is central revocation. Replacement: each unit has its own key pair (already planned), an "owner" unit or phone signs a membership list with a version counter, and units gossip the newest signed list and derive a fresh group key (or use pairwise sessions to distribute one). A lost unit is revoked by publishing a new list; units that are offline learn it when they next meet any member. Without this, a shared static group key means one lost unit compromises the group until every device is re-provisioned by hand.
- Replay and floor spoofing: floor-control messages must be authenticated and carry counters, or any member (or a replaying outsider) can jam the channel with "floor taken". Inside the group, a malicious member can always jam; this is inherent to arbiter-free floor control.
- OTA without a server remains safe if images are signed and fetched from static hosting; the signing key, not the host, is the trust anchor.
- Commercial angle: P2P reduces the operator's liability as a traffic carrier and its bandwidth bill, but support gets harder ("why can't Nana's unit reach ours?") because failures depend on ISPs and routers the operator does not control and cannot observe without telemetry.

### Gaps
- No source retrieved on real attacks against hole-punched IoT devices, or on NZ privacy-law treatment of exposing customers' IP addresses to each other.
- No retrieved reference design for serverless group re-keying on microcontrollers (MLS/TreeKEM is the standards-track answer for groups but an ESP32 implementation was not searched).
